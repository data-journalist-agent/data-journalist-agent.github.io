17From 22 to 279 in two years

18In 2020 the Nepal Himalaya recorded 22 expeditions. 19Two years later, in 2022, it recorded 279 - a 12.7x rebound. 20By 2023 the count was still 274 and by autumn 2024 the slice had already booked another 100. 21This is not a sport recovering; it is an industry being rebuilt at a different scale.

22The same boom that filled this dataset filled the news. 23In spring 2023 Nepal issued a record 478 Everest permits and at least 17 climbers died, making it the deadliest Everest season ever recorded. 24In 2024 Nepal's Supreme Court ordered the government to limit permits.

25And yet success rates climbed too: 80.7% of expeditions summited in 2023 and 81.0% in the partial 2024. 26The mountains have become statistically more survivable for the people on top, even as the absolute body count rose. 27That paradox is the story.

33Three mountains, half the calendar

34Of 480 catalogued peaks in the Nepal Himalaya, three absorbed 49.0% of all 2020-2024 expeditions. 35Everest alone took 21.4% (189 expeditions). 36Ama Dablam, the technical 6,812 m trophy peak in the Khumbu, took 16.7% (147). 37Manaslu, marketed as the "easiest 8000er," took 10.9% (96).

38The next tier - Lhotse, Himlung Himal, Dhaulagiri I, Makalu, Baruntse, Annapurna I, Kangchenjunga - dropped sharply: each under 10% of the total. 39Eight of the world's fourteen 8000ers lie in or partly in Nepal, but climbing tourism is not distributed across them. 40It clusters on the names you have heard of.

44From 93% to 27%

45Among peaks attempted at least ten times, success rates span 93.2% (Ama Dablam) down to 27.3% (Pumori). 46Everest sits near the top at 89.4%, alongside Annapurna I (86.4%) and Lhotse (86.1%). 47Dhaulagiri I, the seventh-highest mountain in the world, sits in the middle at 51.4%.

48What separates the top from the bottom is not the height of the peak. 49It is the infrastructure: which mountains have paid Sherpa rope-fixing teams, fixed oxygen caches, and a known weather-window. 50Ama Dablam is technical but commercially "solved." Pumori is a smaller peak but lacks that machinery. 51The peaks that get climbed are the peaks that have been industrialised.

56When climbers fail, the sky wins

57Across all 882 expeditions, 71.2% ended in success on the main peak and another 2.95% on a sub-peak. 58Of the failure modes, "bad weather (storms, high winds)" was the largest at 8.84% of all expeditions, followed by "bad conditions (deep snow, avalanching)" at 6.12% and "illness, AMS, exhaustion or frostbite" at 2.83%.

59Restricted to the 228 expeditions that did NOT summit, the picture is starker: weather and conditions together account for 57.9% of all failures. 60Pure technical difficulty - the romantic image of the unclimbable cliff - explains just 3.95% of failures, and outright accidents 2.19%.

61In other words, in the modern era of fixed ropes and Sherpa support, climbers don't usually fail because the route beat them. 62They fail because the sky did. 63That is a profound shift from the heroic-failure narrative.

70The 38-point oxygen line

71The strongest binary predictor of summit success in this dataset is whether the expedition used bottled oxygen. 72With oxygen: 94.4% summited (386 of 409). 73Without oxygen: 56.7% (268 of 473). 74A 37.7 percentage-point gap.

75Above 7,500 m the body cannot acclimatize indefinitely; bottled oxygen lowers the effective altitude. 76The gap is not just a chemistry effect, however. 77Oxygen marks a specific style of climbing: large commercial expeditions with Sherpa support, fixed lines, and pre-stocked oxygen caches at high camps.

78Counter-intuitively, oxygen-using expeditions also recorded MORE deaths per expedition: 7.6% vs 1.5% for no-oxygen. 79That is selection bias, not chemistry. 80Oxygen-using expeditions go to Everest and Manaslu where altitude itself is lethal; no-oxygen expeditions cluster on Ama Dablam at 6,812 m. 81The oxygen line marks where the lethal mountains begin.

89The eight 8,000-metre peaks of Nepal

90The eight 8,000-metre peaks in the Nepal Himalaya absorbed 483 of 882 expeditions (54.8%) in 2020-2024. 91Per 1,000 people-on-mountain in this slice, the death rate ranged from 0.0 (Cho Oyu, with 212 climbers and zero deaths) to 7.30 on Everest (4,659 climbers, 34 deaths) and 6.08 on Kangchenjunga (329 climbers, 2 deaths).

92Annapurna I shows up here with a 86.4% success rate and a 2.92 per 1,000 death rate over 22 expeditions - well below its long-run reputation. 93The Himalayan Database itself credits Annapurna with a cumulative 13.42% fatality-to-summit ratio across all history (75 deaths, 559 ascents). 94The 2020-2024 window is a quiet chapter for Annapurna, not a vindication.

95What the slice does show: even within the 8000ers, death rates differ by more than 3x between Everest and Manaslu. 96The mountain you climb matters more than your bottle of oxygen.

105Sherpas: 32% of the deaths

106In 2020-2024, the dataset records 53 deaths total: 36 members and 17 hired staff. 107Sherpas and other hired support make up 32.1% of deaths, while accounting for roughly half of the people on the mountain (6,716 hired vs 7,226 members). 108Per-person death rates: members 4.98 per 1,000, hired 2.53 per 1,000.

109Per-person, members were twice as likely to die as their Sherpas. 110But the absolute count - 17 Sherpa deaths in five years - is the human face of the commercial machinery that delivered 79.2% success rates for client expeditions versus 48.6% for alpine-style teams climbing without hired staff. 111Sherpas turn rope-fixing, oxygen-caching, and load-carrying into routine. 112The cost of that routine is partly absorbed in their fatality count.

118Spring rules, winter punishes

119Climbing is bimodal in Nepal. 120Spring (Mar-May) hosts 52.4% of expeditions, Autumn (Sep-Nov) 44.7%. 121Summer (the monsoon) and Winter together fill the remaining 3%. 122Success rates: Spring 77.1%, Autumn 72.8%, Summer 60.0% (n=5), Winter 38.1%.

123Winter expeditions on Nepal's high peaks are rare and brutal. 124Of 21 winter attempts in this slice, only 8 succeeded. 125Spring's pre-monsoon stable weather plus the famous May summit window explains why the climbing year is built around it.

130Sixty-four nations on the mountain

131Sixty-four distinct principal nationalities appear in the 2020-2024 record. 132Nepal led with 130 expeditions (14.7%), followed by the USA at 117 (13.3%), the UK at 91 (10.3%), India and France tied at 51 each (5.8%). 133Germany, China, Austria, Russia, Japan, Spain rounded out the top ten.

134Nepal's leading position reflects both domestic Nepali teams and the rise of Nepali-led commercial expedition companies. 135The same labour pool that supplies Sherpas now also supplies the operators who employ them.

139112 unclimbed peaks remain

140The peaks_tidy table catalogues 480 peaks, of which 112 (23.3%) remain unclimbed. 141They span 5,407 m to 8,077 m, with a median of 6,452 m - meaning that mountains taller than many Alpine summits have never been climbed by anyone. 142The Kanti/Palchung range holds the most unclimbed peaks (14), followed by Janak/Ohmi Kangri (12), Api/Byas Risi/Guras (11) and Khumbu (11).

143These are not the famous mountains. 144They sit on the western and eastern flanks of Nepal and along the Tibetan border, far from the Lukla airstrip and the Khumbu base camps. 145The frontier of Himalayan climbing has not closed - it has just moved off the postcards.

What we measure now

149Of 882 expeditions in this slice, exactly zero have CLAIMED set to TRUE and only two have DISPUTED set to TRUE. 150Elizabeth Hawley spent fifty years cross-checking summit claims so tenaciously that the climbing community called her "the Sherlock Holmes of the mountaineering world." In the era of GPS, smartphones, and Sherpa eyewitnesses, almost no one tries to fake a summit anymore.

151Hawley's archive, now maintained by the non-profit Himalayan Database, has outlived her by seven years. 152The dataset behind this blog is the proof: a 2020-2024 window through which we can see commercial mountaineering at its largest scale ever, and read its inputs - permits, oxygen bottles, fixed ropes, hired Sherpas - against its outputs - 654 summits, 53 deaths, three peaks absorbing half the calendar.

153What ends today on Everest doesn't end the questions Hawley asked. 154It just changes the form they take. 155Counting climbers used to mean catching liars. 156Now it means catching the system.
