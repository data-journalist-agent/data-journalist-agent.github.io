5The week the satellites lit up

2 634,248 7MODIS satellite fire-pixel detections over Australia in 8 days, 29 Dec 2019 → 5 Jan 2020.

8In the seven days from 29 December 2019 to 5 January 2020, NASA's MODIS satellites returned 34,248 thermal-anomaly pixels over Australia. 9Detections were uneven — 6,936 on 30 December, 7,384 on 4 January, just 867 on 5 January as the file cuts off. 10The hot pixels were not random noise; they tracked the operational tempo of the disaster.

13Fifty-nine percent of those pixels — 20,261 of them — came from a single state. 14Victoria, the second-smallest mainland state, contributed more than triple any other region. 15New South Wales added 13%, Western Australia 11%, Queensland and inland scrub a combined 12%, South Australia 4%, Tasmania just 1%. 16The disaster was not nationally distributed; it was concentrated in a corner.

23Sixty percent of Victoria's fires burned at night

7 2460.6% 25of Victorian MODIS detections were at night — versus ~14% in South Australia and 27% in Western Australia.

26The dangerous hallmark of Black Summer was night activity. 27In Victoria, 60.6% of MODIS detections were night pixels. 28New South Wales ran 53.5% night detections. 29Most other regions sat closer to a normal day-skewed pattern (WA 26.6%, SA 14.3%). 30Night fire is operationally menacing — humidity recovery stops, crews fatigue, fronts continue to advance.

34The fire-radiative-power distribution has a long, heavy right tail. 35Most pixels were modest (the median released 38.7 megawatts), but the top one percent exceeded 1,657 MW and the single hottest pixel released 7,109 MW. 36This is the signature of pyrocumulonimbus events — fires generating their own weather.

37The brightest pixels in the dataset cluster in a small lat-lon box in Victoria's alpine south-east, around -36.7 to -37.7 latitude and 147 to 149 longitude. 38The single hottest detection sat near (-37.74, 149.37) on the night of 29 December, releasing 5,587 MW — that is the East Gippsland fire that, days later, drove around 4,000 trapped residents and tourists onto the beach at Mallacoota waiting for navy evacuation.

41The driest year, almost everywhere

42Pooling the six BoM stations in the dataset, 2019's annual rainfall was 571 mm — just 61% of the 931 mm long-term mean. 43The decline from 2010 was almost monotonic: 1190, 1022, 952, 963, 769, 919, 847, 731, 671, 571. 44Three consecutive sub-average years preceded Black Summer.

48At every individual station, 2019 ranked in the bottom 12 - 15% of years on record. 49In Canberra it was the single driest year in the available 11-year record. 50In Brisbane, 5th of 42 years. 51In Perth, 5th of 52. 52In Melbourne, 7th of 49. 53In Sydney, 19th of 161 — meaning four-fifths of all years recorded since 1858 were wetter.

57But it was the shape of the drought that mattered most. 58Canberra received 1.2 mm in December against an 84.7 mm climatology — a deficit of -83.5 mm. 59Sydney 1.6 against 77.6 mm. 60Melbourne 4.4 against 62.3 mm. 61Brisbane lost 125.7 mm in November alone. 62The 2019 deficit was not spread evenly across the year; it collapsed exactly in the months that should have rewetted the fuel load before summer.

65A century of warming, then 2019

66Pooled across the long-record cities, 2019 sits at 26.4 °C — the highest annual mean daily maximum in the entire 1910–2019 series, and roughly 3 °C above the surrounding decade. 67The shape of the curve is gradual warming through the 20th century followed by a step-function jump in 2019.

80Four million hectares, in one state, on one day

81On the morning of 6 January 2020 — the day this dataset was downloaded — the NSW Rural Fire Service reported 143 declared major incidents. 82Of those, 82 were under control, 30 being controlled, and 31 still out of control. 83The agency was tracking a continuous wave of active calls.

84The 15 largest read like a roll call of Black Summer's worst fires. 85Gospers Mountain, in Wollemi National Park north-west of Sydney, had reached 511,661 hectares — the largest single forest fire from one ignition in Australian history (a lightning strike on 26 October 2019). 86Dunns Road sat at 313,536 ha and was still officially out of control. 87Currowan, Green Wattle Creek, Carrai Creek, Badja Forest Road — every one of the top eight individually exceeded 150,000 hectares.

94What the dataset is, and what it is not

95One discipline this dataset enforces: the satellite hotspots are not fires. 96A MODIS row is a 1 km thermal pixel that was anomalous when the satellite passed overhead. 97Roughly 100 hectares of ground per pixel, but the same fire is re-detected on every overpass and many pixels overlap, so the 34,000 rows cannot be multiplied to a hectare total. 98The official 24 Mha national figure comes from ground accounting, not the satellite.

99The TidyTuesday release itself was not a normal weekly drop. 100Its readme reads as a warning: "PLEASE be cautious when plotting maps of ongoing fires." It was assembled in the middle of an active disaster, three days into the new year, while a country burned. 101This blog uses the same dataset to step back, six years on, and ask what the data — viewed against a longer record — actually showed.

102The data didn't cause Black Summer. 103Climate change, drought cycles, fuel load, and a complex coupled-ocean precondition involving the Indian Ocean Dipole and a Central Pacific El Niño did. 104But on this particular eight-day window, the disaster was already legible to anyone with a satellite, a rainfall gauge, and a temperature thermometer. 105The slow lines on the chart had finally crossed.
