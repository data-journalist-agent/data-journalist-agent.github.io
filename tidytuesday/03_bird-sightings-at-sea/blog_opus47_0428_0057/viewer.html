2Birds Behind the Wake

3Twenty-one years of ten-minute seabird counts, hand-written on ships crossing the Tasman Sea, are now a 12,310-row dataset. 4Here is what they reveal — and what one volunteer named J. 5Jenkins did to make it possible.

016A logbook from the edge of the world

7Between 1969 and 1990, volunteer observers leaned over the rails of cargo ships, fishing trawlers, and research vessels crossing the Tasman Sea, scribbling every bird they saw into pocket notebooks. 8Each entry covered exactly ten minutes — a slice of the open ocean turned into a row of paper. 9The notebooks now live at Te Papa Tongarewa10, the Museum of New Zealand, where staff have transcribed them into the dataset behind this post: 1112,310 ten-minute censuses paired with 1249,019 species observations.

19The geography is unforgiving. 20Records span fifty degrees of latitude and one hundred and thirty degrees of longitude, but the heart of the program is the Tasman Sea and adjacent Southern Ocean — one of the most productive seabird zones on Earth, where the East Australian Current meets cold Subantarctic water at the Subtropical Front and concentrates krill, squid, and fish into albatross-sized meals. 21Almost every census recorded birds: only 1.4% came back blank. 22In these waters, ten minutes was almost never enough time for nothing to happen.

0223What the logbooks could and could not capture

24The data quality is exactly what you would expect from a hand-recorded program: behaviours and locations are nearly perfect, instruments are mostly missing. 25Position, observer, date, time, and behaviour flags are populated in over 2699.9% of censuses. 27But ocean depth is missing from 98.9% of records, sea-surface temperature from 94.4%, atmospheric pressure from 93.7%, and air temperature from 91.6%. 28Anything an observer could note from the deck got noted; anything requiring a gauge below decks usually did not.

31A second quality caveat is taxonomic. 32The species_common_name column was the original log entry — free text written at speed on a moving ship — and 3338.7% 34of those entries could not be resolved to a single species. 35Te Papa staff record those as slash-separated aggregates like "Diomedea epomophora / sanfordi / antipodensis / exulans". 36Six entries also carry the sentinel value 99,999, meaning "we estimated more than one hundred thousand birds and stopped counting" — they have to be excluded from any sum.

0337Albatrosses by encounter, shearwaters by the million

38The dataset has two kinds of "top species" lists, and they tell different stories. 39Ranked by how often a species shows up across the 48,328 species rows, four of the top five entries are wandering albatross 40plumage stages: PL2 (3,104 records), PL3 (2,885), PL1 (1,818), PL4 (1,475). 41Cape petrel (2,936), grey-faced petrel (2,597), and unidentified prion (2,094) round out the encounters.

43Re-ranking by total individuals counted flips the picture entirely. 44Short-tailed shearwater 45alone accounts for 482,558 of the 1.34 million birds counted across the program — 4636% of every individual ever logged 47— followed by unidentified prion (226,500) and sooty shearwater (174,329). 48Albatrosses do not even reach the top fifteen by birds-counted. 49Albatrosses are seen often but in small numbers; shearwaters are seen rarely but in feeding aggregations of thousands. 50Two top-tens, two ecologies.

0452The geography of effort

53Flatten the 12,310 censuses onto a 5°×5° grid and the densest cell sits at roughly 40°S, 170°E with 541,293 censuses 55(10.5% of program effort) and 210,861 individual birds counted; the second is just below it at 45°S, 170°E. 56These cells sit in the southern Tasman Sea and the Subtropical Front off New Zealand's South Island — exactly where royal albatross, white-capped mollymawk, and sooty shearwater forage between breeding bouts on New Zealand's offshore islands.

58By latitude band, two-thirds of effort (5966.0%60) sits between 30°S and 40°S — the warmer Tasman waters around the North Island; another 28.6% sits on the Subtropical Front (40-50°S). 61Together those two bands hold 94.6% of all program effort. 62Antarctic-water records south of 50°S (590 censuses) and tropical records north of 30°S (70 censuses) are exotic outliers in this dataset.

0563The shape of time: a five-year pause and a 1980s boom

64The number of censuses per year does not look like a smooth trend; it looks like a heartbeat that stopped and restarted. 65After 243 censuses in 1969 the program effectively paused — 661970 through 1974 inclusive recorded zero. 67Activity resumed in 1975 with 202 censuses, ramped up through the late 1970s, and peaked in 1987 at 1,634. 68The four years 1984–1987 alone hold 5,847 of the 12,310 censuses — 6947.5% of all program effort. 70By 1989-1990, effort had collapsed to 26 and 20 censuses.

73The shape of this curve is a story about volunteer observers and ship schedules, not about birds. 74The Australasian Seabird Mapping Scheme depended on people who happened to be on a vessel that happened to be passing through; pause the recruitment, and the data goes flat. 75Across all years effort is reasonably balanced across seasons, and 98.4% of censuses were full ten-minute counts rather than partial observations. 76When observers were active, they were rigorous; the gaps come from above, not from the protocol.

0677Following the ship: a behavioural fingerprint

78Every species record carries a set of TRUE/FALSE behaviour flags, and the most striking is following_ship. 79Roughly a quarter of all 48,328 species records are flagged following_ship (8024.5%81) — almost the same as flying_past (24.3%) — followed by accompanying (13.8%), feeding (7.5%), and sitting_on_water (7.2%). 82Active natural feeding is logged in only 2.0% of records.

Who follows? 83Of the 11,819 ship-following observations, wandering albatrosses across plumage stages PL1-PL4 plus AD and SUBAD account for 845,491 records — 46.5% of every ship-following event 85in two decades — followed by Cape petrel (1,021) and black-browed albatross adults (823).

0795One logbook, twenty-six hands, one dominant voice

96The final feature of the dataset is its most human. 97The entire 21-year record was produced by 26 unique observers — and one of them, J. 98Jenkins, recorded 6,583 censuses, 53.5% of the program. 99The top five (Jenkins, N. 100Cheshire 1,462, A. 101Baines 1,250, J.D. 102Cleaver 1,023, M.J. 103Carter 747) account for 89.9% of all effort; the top ten cover 96.4%.

10
104Top 10 observers by number of censuses. 105The Tasman Sea seabird record is, in a real sense, mostly one person's logbook.

106Ninety-four per cent of the censuses were taken from a ship that was steaming or sailing; only 0.5% from a line-fishing boat and 0.4% from a trawler — meaning fishery-discard interactions are massively under-sampled relative to their ecological importance. 107Wind matters too, but only a little. 108Where wind class was logged, Beaufort 2-5 (light to fresh breezes) covers 73% of those censuses; gales are rare and the one hurricane census recorded zero birds. 109Median birds-per-census is remarkably stable across the breeze range — six birds at calm, eight to ten birds from light breeze through gale. 110Weather is a poor predictor of how many birds an observer saw, because two effects cancel: high winds mean better albatross soaring (more birds aloft) but worse visibility (fewer birds detected).

111What we are left with is a baseline. 112A pre-1990 portrait of a Southern Ocean seabird community, hand-written in pencil on a moving ship, before the worst of the longline-bycatch crisis and before the Tasman Sea warmed measurably. 113Every conservation policy of the last three decades — ACAP, area closures, line-weighting regulations — is being measured against logbooks like this one.

Source. 114TidyTuesday 2026-04-14: At-Sea Observations of Seabirds 1969-1990, Te Papa Tongarewa via OBIS & data.govt.nz.

Caveats.

  • 115The dataset is opportunistic effort by 26 volunteers, with 53.5% from one observer; geographic and temporal patterns reflect ship routes and observer availability, not bird abundance.
  • 1161970-1974 contain zero censuses (program pause); the 1984-1987 peak is an effort spike, not a population spike.
  • 11738.7% of records are taxonomic aggregates (slash-separated). 118Species-level claims should be read as "log entry", not "identified species".
  • 119Six counts at the 99,999 sentinel value are excluded from total-bird sums.
  • 120Wind-vs-bird-count comparisons are confounded: high winds reduce detection, and detection probability moves opposite to bird behaviour at low wind.
