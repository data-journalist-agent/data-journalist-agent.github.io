401 — Hook5Three categories of animal

6Of the 29,787 animals that walked, were carried, or were trapped through the doors of Long Beach Animal Care Services between 2017 and 2024, only 7,660 arrived with a name a human had given them. 7That is 25.7% — barely one in four. 8Another 9,965 animals (33.5%) start with an asterisk in the data file: a name made up by shelter staff at intake. 9The remaining 12,162 (40.8%) have no name at all.

17The asterisk is not decoration. 18The TidyTuesday data dictionary tells you exactly what it means: 19"Animals with * are given by shelter staff." 20It is a footnote about formatting. 21It is also, as it turns out, the cleanest single proxy for human attachment in the entire dataset.

22Cats and dogs that arrive with an owner-given name go home to their owner 41.3% of the time. 23Cats and dogs whose names start with an asterisk go home to that owner 0.8% of the time. 24The asterisk is the difference between a lost dog and a stray.

2802 — Context29A shelter, not just a cat-and-dog place

30Cats and dogs together make up 80.4% of the shelter's intake, but the rest of the building is busy too. 31Birds account for 7.0% of records, "wild" animals — raccoons, opossums, skunks, hawks — for 4.7%, "other" small mammals and exotic pets for 4.5%, with rabbits, reptiles, guinea pigs, livestock, and even three amphibians filling out the long tail.

32Long Beach Animal Care Services is open-admission and municipally contracted: it cannot turn animals away the way a private rescue can. 33It also serves four neighbouring cities — Cerritos, Signal Hill, Los Alamitos, and Seal Beach — which together send 12.3% of the intake. 34Long Beach itself is 85.9%. 35The dataset is, in other words, a regional triage hub: companion animals from five cities, plus the wildlife that those five cities cross paths with.

4003 — The pandemic VTwo quiet years

41Annual intake at LBACS fell off a cliff in 2020. 42From a baseline of 4,826 in 2017, it dropped to 2,421 in 2020 and an even-lower 1,842 in 2021 — 38% of the 2017 number. 43The same shape shows up in U.S. 44shelter data nationwide: stray intake fell 28% between 2019 and 2020 because stay-at-home orders meant fewer people walking past loose animals to find them.

45By 2024, intake had climbed back to 4,411, essentially the pre-pandemic level. 46The Long Beach intake curve is a textbook pandemic V: nothing about animal welfare changed, but the world got quieter for two years and so did the shelter.

47At the quarterly resolution the shape is sharper still. 48Intakes ran 1,200 to 1,500 per quarter through most of 2017–2019. 49Q1 2020 — the first lockdown quarter — collapsed to 452, and intake stayed below 800 a quarter all the way through Q1 2022. 50The recovery is fully visible by Q2 2024, which clocks 1,587 — the busiest quarter in the dataset, and the reason for the next chart.

5504 — Seasonality56Kitten season is real

57If you want to know why animal shelters obsess over foster networks, look at May. 58In May, LBACS takes in 901 underage kittens — cats arriving in the "under age/weight" intake condition, which mostly means neonates that cannot yet eat without help. 59In December, that number is 82. 60May is more than ten times worse than December.

61The whole April-to-October stretch averages 600+ underage kittens a month, and overall intake follows the same curve: June peaks at 3,297, December bottoms at 1,633. 62Kitten season is the single largest seasonal stress on the shelter's medical and foster systems, and it is the operational reason that an open-admission shelter can never rest from May through October.

6705 — The turn682024 was the best year. 69The save rate says it was just average.

70The City's January 2025 press release was right: 2024 was a record. Adoptions hit 1,463 — 3.15× the 2018 baseline of 464, and well past the strategic plan's 1,500-pet placement target when foster and offsite events are counted. 72Adoptions have grown every year since 2021.

73But adoption is not the only outcome that matters, and the live-release rate — the metric that defines a "no-kill" shelter, by Best Friends' 90%-over-twelve-months convention — tells a flatter story. 74Cats and dogs leaving the shelter alive: 81.3% in 2017, 78.3% in 2018, 86.9% in 2019 (the year the city formally adopted its Compassion Saves model), 92.2% in 2020 (the only year above the no-kill line), then 84.7%, 83.5%, 82.8%, 84.6%. 75The 2024 number is barely a percentage point above the 2017 number.

76More animals saved than ever before, at the same percentage as the year before.

77The reason is not that fewer animals are being saved. 78It is that more animals are walking in. 79Total euthanasia for all species fell from 1,001 in 2017 to 581 in 2019 — a 42% drop in the year Compassion Saves was adopted — and held below 750 every year after. 80But intake recovered while euthanasia stayed roughly flat, so the percentage stuck. 81The absolute story is cleaner than the rate. 82(The data shows this trend coincides with the Compassion Saves policy and the pandemic — it cannot prove either is the cause.)

9306 — Species94What you are decides where you go

95Live-release by species, all years pooled: dogs 92.5%, cats 77.7%, rabbits 81.1%, reptiles 92.2%, guinea pigs 96.5%, birds 68.9%, "wild" 32.4%. 96The 90% no-kill benchmark, designed for cats and dogs, treats those two species as a combined target. 97Dogs comfortably clear it on their own. 98Cats fall about twelve points short.

99Live-release rate by species

100Dashed line is the 90% no-kill benchmark. 101Wild animals are euthanised most often because they typically arrive non-survivable; reading their rate alongside cat & dog rates is a category error.

102Where you go after the shelter is also species-specific. 103Dogs predominantly go home — 29.7% return to owner — and 26.6% are adopted; only 6.7% are euthanised. 104Cats almost never go home (2.2% return-to-owner) but 9.6% leave through TNR pathways: shelter-neuter-return, community cat, trap-neuter-release. 105Birds usually transfer to a rescue partner. 106Wild animals are euthanised 62.2% of the time, but they typically arrive injured beyond rehabilitation. 107Reading those numbers as a single "save rate" papers over five different problems.

11107 — Triage112The strongest predictor sits at the front door

113The strongest single predictor of a cat or dog's outcome is none of the things you'd expect — not species, not year, not owner-named-or-not. It is the intake_condition 114column: the staff's triage code at the door. 115Cats and dogs entering as "normal" leave alive 95.3% of the time. 116"Under age/weight" — the neonatal kitten category — drops to 78.5%. 117"Injured severe" falls to 30.7%, "ill severe" to 24.9%.

118At the other end of the table, "fractious" cats — too unsocialised to be handled — leave alive 89.9% of the time, almost all of them through TNR. 119"Welfare seizures" — animals removed from cruelty cases — sit at 100% live release on a small sample of 65, consistent with deliberate placement. 120The shelter's headline number is the average of these subgroups, weighted by who walks in. 121A shelter that takes more severely-injured animals will have a lower headline rate even if its medical work is identical to a shelter that takes only "normal" intake.

12508 — Surrender126Why people give up pets

127The 2,615 owner-surrender records contain the saddest open-text field in the dataset. 128After excluding the third with no reason recorded, the top four reasons are housing- and money-driven: "owner problem" 129(405), "move" 130(231), "landlord" 131(133), and "cost" 132(127). 133Together they account for half of all owner surrenders with a reason — a quiet ledger of California rental policy and pet-deposit math. "New baby," 134the cliché reason, is one of the smallest categories at 13.

138Length of stay is shaped by the same gravitational pulls. 139Dogs returned to their owners go home in a median of one day. 140Adoptions take a median of 27 days, the slowest live exit, because they require an adopter to come, choose, sign, pay, and leave with the animal. 141Rescues and transfers move in 6 days; euthanasia has a 0-day median because much of it is "euthenasia required" day-of-intake for non-survivable wildlife or trauma. 142Adoption is the slow, miraculous path; everything else is fast.

14609 — Geography147Where the animals come from

148The geography of intake reads like a map of where Long Beach is dense and where Long Beach is wealthy. 149The 90805 ZIP — North Long Beach, including the Bixby Knolls borders — alone produces 5,672 intakes, 19% of all rows where a ZIP can be parsed. 15090813 (Central Long Beach / Cambodia Town) follows with 3,024, then 90806 (Wrigley) at 2,583 and 90815 (Los Altos) at 2,334.

151One artefact deserves a footnote: the densest single map cell in the dataset, with 560 intakes, sits at the shelter's own coordinates inside El Dorado Park. 152Many free-text crossing addresses default to the building when no precise pickup location was recorded. 153The real intake map starts after that one bin is removed.

169And the reason all of those names matter is that the asterisk pattern is intake-type specific in a way that carries the whole story. 170Owner-surrender intakes are 76.8% owner-named: someone signed the animal in by name. "Wildlife" 171intakes are 97.0% blank: nobody names an opossum at intake. 172"Trap-neuter-return" 173intakes are 68.5% staff-named: the colony cat pattern, where staff label the same cat over multiple visits. 174Each intake type has its own naming fingerprint, and the fingerprint maps cleanly onto the outcome the animal will eventually have.

17811 — Close179The map and the asterisk

180The cleanest summary the dataset offers is also the simplest. 181Owner-named cats and dogs leave alive 93.2% of the time. 182Staff-named cats and dogs leave alive 93.2% of the time. 183Blank-named animals — overwhelmingly wildlife — leave alive 61.7% of the time, but "leaving alive" isn't really the goal for a hawk with a broken wing. 184The 90% benchmark applies to a population of cats and dogs, and within that population, the asterisk does not change a single life.

185What it does change is what the shelter has to do to get there. 186Owner-named animals get reunited; staff-named ones get adopted; both pathways take roughly the same fraction home. 187The asterisk is the column that tells you which path the animal is on the moment the door opens.

188Long Beach hit a record adoption year in 2024, and the city was right to celebrate. 189The save rate did not move. 190It will not move much until intake stops growing, or until the system finds a way to convert the long medical tail — the "ill severe" 24.9%, the "injured severe" 30.7% — into a different kind of story. 191Until then, the shelter's annual report is two numbers, both true: more saved than ever before, and the same percentage as the year before.
