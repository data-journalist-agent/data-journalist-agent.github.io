Hook

8One Super Bowl ad in this dataset has been watched 176 million times on YouTube. 9It is not the Budweiser Clydesdales. 10It is not Apple's "1984," which is from before this data starts anyway. 11It is "Sling Baby" — a 2012 Doritos commercial in which a baby slingshots itself across a backyard to grab a chip out of an old man's hand. 12It was made by a fan, not an agency.

2 13176,373,378 YouTube views — for one Doritos ad about a baby in a slingshot. 154,262 times the median ad in the dataset.

16"Sling Baby" alone accounts for 54.2% of all YouTube views in the dataset. 17It is 4,262 times the median ad. 18The top 10 ads together account for 88.8% of total views. The other 234 commercials — including most of what your friends will tell you they remember from the Super Bowl — share what's left.

20The slingshot baby is a clue, not a punchline. 21Between 2006 and 2016, Doritos ran a contest called 22Crash the Super Bowl23: anyone could submit a thirty-second ad, and the winners actually aired. 24Across eight editions, fans uploaded more than 36,000 entries. 25Every Crash ad that aired finished in the top five of USA Today's Ad Meter. 26So the most-watched Super Bowl ad of the dataset is also a piece of evidence that the genre has been quietly running an open audition for itself.

29Seven boxes, two dominate

30FiveThirtyEight watched 233 commercials from the 10 brands that aired the most Super Bowl spots this century — 244 unique ads in this distribution, 21 years of February — and asked seven yes/no questions of each one. 31Was it trying to be funny? 32Did it show the product quickly? Was it patriotic? 33Did a celebrity appear? Was there danger? An animal? Sex?

34Two of the seven traits dominate. 3568.9% of ads are funny; 68.0% show the product quickly. The next three — animals (36.5%), danger (30.7%), celebrity (27.9%) — cluster in the middle. 37Use_sex sits at 25.8%. 38Patriotic is the rarest of the seven, on just 16.8% of ads. 39The average Super Bowl ad is more likely to feature a celebrity than to wave an American flag.

The traits stack. 42The mean ad checks 2.75 boxes; the median checks 3. 43Only four ads in twenty-one years (1.6%) check zero boxes — pure exposition, no animal, no joke. 44Two ads (0.8%) check six or seven boxes. 45The Super Bowl ad recipe, in other words, is not a single ingredient but a small handful combined.

48Each brand wears a different costume

49Bud Light is the volume king of the Super Bowl, with 60 ads in 21 years — one every four months on average. 50Budweiser is second with 43. 51Together, Anheuser-Busch's two beer brands account for 42.2% of every ad in this dataset. The other eight brands — Pepsi, Doritos, Hyundai, Coca-Cola, Kia, E-Trade, Toyota, and the NFL itself — split the other 141 ads.

53But Bud Light's signature isn't the volume. It's the rigidity. 5495% of Bud Light's 60 ads are coded funny. 5581.7% show the product quickly. 5676.7% are both. Zero — literally none — are neither. 58There is no "serious" Bud Light Super Bowl ad in 21 years. 59Every February, the brand walks out wearing the same costume.

62Doritos goes harder: 96% of its 25 ads are funny, and exactly one in 21 years isn't. 63The single non-funny Doritos Super Bowl spot is the exception that proves the rule. 64And given the brand's open-submission Crash the Super Bowl program, it's also true that the comedy is being co-written by the audience.

65The cleanest counterexample is the NFL itself. 66Across 11 league-run Super Bowl ads, 0% involve danger, 0% involve animals, 0% use sex. 67The NFL is the only brand to hit zero on three traits at once. 68It substitutes celebrity (73%) and patriotism (36%): the Super Bowl League pitches itself as PR rather than a product.

69Each of the ten brands has a fingerprint. 70Pepsi is the celebrity brand (64% celebrity, 44% sex). 71Coca-Cola is the warmer one (57% animals, 5% sex, only 38% funny). 72Kia is the most provocative car brand (46% sex, 39% animals). 73Once you have the seven traits, you can almost identify the brand from the recipe alone.

76The 21-year drift

Cut the dataset in half — 2000-2009 versus 2010-2020 — and the Super Bowl ad of the second decade is a different animal. 78Funny ads fall from 83.8% of all spots to 56.4%, a 27.4-percentage-point drop. 79Use_sex falls 13.8 points. 80Danger drops 8 points. 81Going the other way: patriotic climbs 12.7 points (9.9% to 22.6%), celebrity climbs 8.2 points, animals tick up 5.8.

82Percentage-point change in each trait between 2000-2009 and 2010-2020. 83Negative = the trait fell; positive = the trait rose.

84Within those changes, the cleanest single-trait collapse is sex. 85In 2000-2004, 38.9% of these brands' Super Bowl ads used sex to sell. 86In 2016-2020, 7.1%. 87That is a 31.7-percentage-point fall, an 82% relative decline. 88Use_sex hit literal zero in both 2017 and 2020. You do not have to pick a single explanation — #MeToo, audience shift, agency self-censorship, the rise of social-media accountability — to see that whatever the reason, the entire industry agreed at roughly the same time.

98The patriotic spike falls in the wrong place

99If you had to guess when patriotic Super Bowl advertising peaked, the obvious answer is wrong. The reflex says February 2002 — the first Super Bowl after September 11. 101Budweiser sent the Clydesdales bowing toward the Manhattan skyline. 102The flag came out everywhere. 103The era of patriotic Super Bowl ads, surely, started there.

9

Guess first

104Which year had the highest share of patriotic Super Bowl ads?
 

The data says no. 105In 2000-2001, just 4.8% of these brands' Super Bowl ads were patriotic. 106In the post-9/11 window of 2002-2004, the share crawls up to 6.1% — a 1.3-percentage-point bump that barely registers. The actual all-time high is 2017, at 60% — three out of every five spots. 108The 2017-2018 window averages 42.1% patriotic, by far the highest in the dataset.

111And patriotic ads earn it: their median YouTube view count is 74,820, almost twice the 39,730 median for non-patriotic ads. 112By contrast, funny vs not-funny is a wash at the median. 113Putting a celebrity on screen actually slightly lowers the median view count. 114The trait that travels best on YouTube is the flag.

118The strongest negative correlation in the data

119Of all 21 trait pairs in the data, the largest negative correlation is funny vs patriotic (phi = -0.384). 120The pairs almost never share a 30-second slot: a patriotic ad is dramatically less likely to be a comedy. 121The only other strongly negative pair is celebrity vs animals (-0.186) — celebrities and pets really do compete for the camera.

125On the positive side, the strongest pairings are funny + danger (+0.275) and funny + use_sex (+0.275) — the genre's permission to be edgy is granted by being funny first. 126So the formula change of the 2010s isn't just "less of one trait, more of another." It is a wholesale switch of comedic register: the comedy/edge cluster fading out, and the celebrity/flag cluster coming in.

129The 30-second polygraph

130A 30-second slot at Super Bowl XXXIV in 2000 cost about $2.1 million. By Super Bowl LIV in 2020, $5.4 million — more than double. 132Across the 21 years in this dataset, the price-per-second floor went up about 2.6x. 133The brands paying it kept showing up: 11.6 ads per year on average from these ten alone, with a low of 5 in 2017 and a peak of 15 in 2009, 2012, 2013, and 2019.

134What they bought, when you look at the recipe, looks remarkably uniform. 135The single most-common combination of traits is the bare 'funny + show product quickly' pair, which appears in 23 ads. Add danger — 'funny + show product quickly + danger' — and you get the next 15. 137The top ten recipes account for the same ground over and over.

139But within that uniform rhythm, the registers drift. 140Less sex, less comedy, more flag, more celebrity. 141A baby fired out of a slingshot in 2012; an immigrant brewer's grandson in 2017; the entire NFL coaching staff bowing to a CGI eagle by the end of the 2020s. 142The Super Bowl ad isn't a constant. 143It's a 30-second polygraph the country submits to, once a year, at five million dollars a question.
