## Story Spine
**Core claim**: The Super Bowl ad of the 2010s is markedly less funny, less sexual, and more patriotic than the Super Bowl ad of the 2000s — and the canonical "post-9/11 patriotic ad boom" never actually happened in the data; the patriotic surge is a 2017 phenomenon.

**Tension**: Most viewers assume the Super Bowl ad has a fixed formula — funny, animal, beer. The trait data says the formula has been quietly drifting for two decades. And the patriotic spike falls in the wrong place.

**Payoff**: The reader leaves understanding that "Super Bowl ads" is not a constant — it is a slow-motion mood reading of corporate America, taken once a year at the most expensive TV slot in the world. The data is a 21-year polygraph, and 2017 is the year the needle jumped.

## Sections

### edt_01: Hook — The most-watched Super Bowl ad is a sneeze-pranking baby
**Evidence**: ana_10, ana_11 | **Context**: det_05, det_09

[ana_10] One Super Bowl ad in this dataset has been watched 176 million times on YouTube. It is not the Budweiser Clydesdales. It is not Apple's "1984," which is from before this data starts anyway. It is "Sling Baby" — a 2012 Doritos commercial in which a baby slingshots itself across a backyard to grab a chip out of an old man's hand. It was made by a fan, not an agency.

[ana_11] "Sling Baby" alone accounts for 54.2% of all YouTube views in the dataset. It is 4,262 times the median ad. The top 10 ads together account for 88.8% of total views. The other 234 commercials — including most of what your friends will tell you they remember from the Super Bowl — share what's left.

[det_05, editorial] The slingshot baby is a clue, not a punchline. Between 2006 and 2016, Doritos ran a contest called Crash the Super Bowl: anyone could submit a thirty-second ad, and the winners actually aired. Across eight editions, fans uploaded more than 36,000 entries. Every Crash ad that aired finished in the top five of USA Today's Ad Meter. So the most-watched Super Bowl ad of the dataset is also a piece of evidence that the genre has been quietly running an open audition for itself.

### edt_02: The seven boxes
**Evidence**: ana_01, ana_02, ana_03 | **Context**: det_01, det_06

[det_01, ana_01] FiveThirtyEight watched 233 commercials from the 10 brands that aired the most Super Bowl spots this century — 244 unique ads in this distribution, 21 years of February — and asked seven yes/no questions of each one. Was it trying to be funny? Did it show the product quickly? Was it patriotic? Did a celebrity appear? Was there danger? An animal? Sex?

[ana_02] Two of the seven traits dominate. 68.9% of ads are funny; 68.0% show the product quickly. The next three — animals (36.5%), danger (30.7%), celebrity (27.9%) — cluster in the middle. Use_sex sits at 25.8%. Patriotic is the rarest of the seven, on just 16.8% of ads. The average Super Bowl ad is more likely to feature a celebrity than to wave an American flag.

[ana_03] The traits stack. The mean ad checks 2.75 boxes; the median checks 3. Only four ads in twenty-one years (1.6%) check zero boxes — pure exposition, no animal, no joke. Two ads (0.8%) check six or seven boxes. The Super Bowl ad recipe, in other words, is not a single ingredient but a small handful combined.

### edt_03: Three brands, three different recipes
**Evidence**: ana_04, ana_06, ana_13, ana_14, ana_18 | **Context**: det_04, det_05

[ana_04] Bud Light is the volume king of the Super Bowl, with 60 ads in 21 years — one every four months on average. Budweiser is second with 43. Together, Anheuser-Busch's two beer brands account for 42.2% of every ad in this dataset. The other eight brands — Pepsi, Doritos, Hyundai, Coca-Cola, Kia, E-Trade, Toyota, and the NFL itself — split the other 141 ads.

[ana_13] But Bud Light's signature isn't the volume. It's the rigidity. 95% of Bud Light's 60 ads are coded funny. 81.7% show the product quickly. 76.7% are both. Zero — literally none — are neither. There is no "serious" Bud Light Super Bowl ad in 21 years. Every February, the brand walks out wearing the same costume.

[ana_18] Doritos goes harder: 96% of its 25 ads are funny, and exactly one in 21 years isn't. The single non-funny Doritos Super Bowl spot is the exception that proves the rule. And given the brand's open-submission Crash the Super Bowl program, it's also true that the comedy is being co-written by the audience.

[ana_14] The cleanest counterexample is the NFL itself. Across 11 league-run Super Bowl ads, 0% involve danger, 0% involve animals, 0% use sex. The NFL is the only brand to hit zero on three traits at once. It substitutes celebrity (73%) and patriotism (36%): the Super Bowl League pitches itself as PR rather than a product.

[ana_06] Each of the ten brands has a fingerprint. Pepsi is the celebrity brand (64% celebrity, 44% sex). Coca-Cola is the warmer one (57% animals, 5% sex, only 38% funny). Kia is the most provocative car brand (46% sex, 39% animals). Once you have the seven traits, you can almost identify the brand from the recipe alone.

### edt_04: The 21-year drift — funny is down, patriotic is up
**Evidence**: ana_07, ana_08, ana_09 | **Context**: det_06

[ana_08] Cut the dataset in half — 2000-2009 versus 2010-2020 — and the Super Bowl ad of the second decade is a different animal. Funny ads fall from 83.8% of all spots to 56.4%, a 27.4-percentage-point drop. Use_sex falls 13.8 points. Danger drops 8 points. Going the other way: patriotic climbs 12.7 points (9.9% to 22.6%), celebrity climbs 8.2 points, animals tick up 5.8.

[ana_09] Within those changes, the cleanest single-trait collapse is sex. In 2000-2004, 38.9% of these brands' Super Bowl ads used sex to sell. In 2016-2020, 7.1%. That is a 31.7-percentage-point fall, an 82% relative decline. Use_sex hit literal zero in both 2017 and 2020. You do not have to pick a single explanation — #MeToo, audience shift, agency self-censorship, the rise of social-media accountability — to see that whatever the reason, the entire industry agreed at roughly the same time.

[ana_07, editorial] Plot the seven traits year-by-year and the lines slowly cross. Funny used to be a 90-100% trait in the early 2000s; it dropped to 20% in 2017. Patriotic went the opposite way: 0% in 2000, 60% in 2017. The graph is the polygraph.

### edt_05: The patriotic spike was 2017, not 9/11
**Evidence**: ana_17, ana_15 | **Context**: det_08, det_03

[editorial] If you had to guess when patriotic Super Bowl advertising peaked, the obvious answer is wrong. The reflex says February 2002 — the first Super Bowl after September 11. Budweiser sent the Clydesdales bowing toward the Manhattan skyline. The flag came out everywhere. The era of patriotic Super Bowl ads, surely, started there.

[ana_17] The data says no. In 2000-2001, just 4.8% of these brands' Super Bowl ads were patriotic. In the post-9/11 window of 2002-2004, the share crawls up to 6.1% — a 1.3-percentage-point bump that barely registers. The actual all-time high is 2017, at 60% — three out of every five spots. The 2017-2018 window averages 42.1% patriotic, by far the highest in the dataset.

[ana_15, editorial] And patriotic ads earn it: their median YouTube view count is 74,820, almost twice the 39,730 median for non-patriotic ads. By contrast, funny vs not-funny is a wash at the median. Putting a celebrity on screen actually slightly lowers the median view count. The trait that travels best on YouTube is the flag.

### edt_06: The strongest negative correlation in the data is funny versus flag
**Evidence**: ana_16 | **Context**: det_06

[ana_16] Of all 21 trait pairs in the data, the largest negative correlation is funny vs patriotic (phi = -0.384). The pairs almost never share a 30-second slot: a patriotic ad is dramatically less likely to be a comedy. The only other strongly negative pair is celebrity vs animals (-0.186) — celebrities and pets really do compete for the camera.

[ana_16, editorial] On the positive side, the strongest pairings are funny + danger (+0.275) and funny + use_sex (+0.275) — the genre's permission to be edgy is granted by being funny first. So the formula change of the 2010s isn't just "less of one trait, more of another." It is a wholesale switch of comedic register: the comedy/edge cluster fading out, and the celebrity/flag cluster coming in.

### edt_07: Close — the 30-second polygraph
**Evidence**: ana_05, ana_12 | **Context**: det_02, det_09

[det_02] A 30-second slot at Super Bowl XXXIV in 2000 cost about $2.1 million. By Super Bowl LIV in 2020, $5.4 million — more than double. Across the 21 years in this dataset, the price-per-second floor went up about 2.6x. The brands paying it kept showing up: 11.6 ads per year on average from these ten alone, with a low of 5 in 2017 and a peak of 15 in 2009, 2012, 2013, and 2019.

[ana_12] What they bought, when you look at the recipe, looks remarkably uniform. The single most-common combination of traits is the bare 'funny + show product quickly' pair, which appears in 23 ads. Add danger — 'funny + show product quickly + danger' — and you get the next 15. The top ten recipes account for the same ground over and over.

[editorial] But within that uniform rhythm, the registers drift. Less sex, less comedy, more flag, more celebrity. A baby fired out of a slingshot in 2012; an immigrant brewer's grandson in 2017; the entire NFL coaching staff bowing to a CGI eagle by the end of the 2020s. The Super Bowl ad isn't a constant. It's a 30-second polygraph the country submits to, once a year, at five million dollars a question.
