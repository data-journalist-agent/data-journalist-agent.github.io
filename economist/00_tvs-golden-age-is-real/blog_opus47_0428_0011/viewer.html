2TV's golden age is real — by the numbers

3Yes, prestige drama really did get better. 4But the rise hides almost entirely at the top of the distribution, where the elite tier multiplied roughly tenfold while everyone else barely budged.

52,266 IMDb-rated American TV-drama seasons · 1990–2018 · Source: The Economist / IMDb

The flat-mean trap

6In 1990 the average IMDb rating for an American TV drama season was 7.94. 7In 2018 it was 8.08. 8That's a gain of 0.14 of a rating point in 28 years — barely visible to the naked eye and well inside the noise of any single year. 9Anyone arguing that television has dramatically improved on the strength of mean ratings alone is fighting a losing battle.

10So why does almost every critic agree that we are living through a golden age of television? Why did The Economist 11in November 2018 publish a chart with the unequivocal title "TV's golden age is real"? 12The answer is in where you look.

16The prestige tier exploded

19Slice the same dataset at the 9.0-out-of-10 line — the threshold above which IMDb voters effectively label a season as exceptional — and the picture changes completely. 20Through the entire late 1990s and most of the 2000s, that tier is essentially empty: 1999, 2000, 2001, 2002, and 2003 each produced zero seasons rated 9.0 or higher. 21Then in 2014 the count jumped to 13, in 2015 to 14, and in 2017 the dataset peaks at 16 prestige seasons in a single year.

22By share, 2014 had 7.83% of all its drama seasons cracking the 9.0 line; the 1990s and early 2000s mostly produced 0–4%. 23The rate at which prestige TV is made roughly tenfolded.

28That growth happened against a separate explosion in raw output. 29There were 207 drama seasons in the entire 1990s. 30There were 1,468 in just the nine years from 2010 to 2018 — seven times as many. 31FX chief John Landgraf had a name for this in 2015: Peak TV. 32The dataset shows both halves of his thesis — more shows, and a denser top.

33What the top of the chart looks like

34If you pull the twenty highest-rated seasons in the entire 28-year dataset, the names you'd expect cluster at the top. 35Breaking Bad's final season scored 9.55 on a 18.95% vote share — the largest sample of any season in its show. 36Game of Thrones season 6 (9.49) and season 4 (9.43). 37True Detective season 1 (9.33). 38Sherlock season 2 (9.32). 39BoJack Horseman season 5 (9.47).

40But the very top has some quieter entries: Parenthood's first season in 1990 holds the dataset's all-time-high 9.68, and Touched by an Angel's fifth season scores 9.60. 41Both have vote shares under 2%. 42The pattern repeats: Are You Afraid of the Dark? 43season 3 (9.43, 2.6% share), Santa Barbara season 1 (9.40, 0.14% share). 44These are the data's reminder that "highest-rated" is not the same as "most-watched"; small fan bases vote loud.

45The shape of the full distribution makes the point: out of 2,266 seasons, only 93 ever cross 9.0, and just four reach 9.5. 46The prestige tier exists because it is rare.

51Sticking the landing

53One pattern shows up in almost every prestige drama: ratings rise into season five and then fall. 54Pooling all 293 shows with at least three seasons in the dataset, the mean season-1 rating is 8.13, climbs to a peak of 8.32 by season 5, then drops to 7.91 by season 12. 55The cliché that shows go downhill after a few years is true on average — but only after season five, not before.

78Where the rise lives, by genre

79Decompose the 1990s-to-2010s rating drift by genre and the rise looks broad but uneven. 80Adventure rose 0.66 points; Action rose 0.43; Comedy rose 0.34; Crime rose 0.22; Drama itself rose 0.28. 81Two genres actually fell: Sci-Fi by 0.16 and Thriller by 0.29.

82So the prestige era did not simply lift "drama" — it lifted dramas with genre flavor on top: action-dramas, adventure-dramas, crime-dramas. 83The shows readers think of as defining the era — The Sopranos, The Wire, Breaking Bad, Game of Thrones — sit in those exact intersections.

87What the algorithm hides

88Every number in this piece comes from IMDb's weighted vote average, an algorithm IMDb deliberately keeps secret to deter brigading. 89A "9.55" is not a simple mean; it is the algorithm's filtered consensus. 90That filter is doing more smoothing on a 2017 season with millions of votes than on a 1992 season with a few thousand.

12 91deliberately undisclosed 92IMDb's exact rating algorithm — a guard against vote brigading. 93Numbers in this piece are weighted averages, not raw user means.

94IMDb voters self-select. 95They skew young, online, and engaged. 96The trend documented here is a rise in 97IMDb-voter consensus98, which is correlated with — but not identical to — "objective" television quality. 99Three of the dataset's all-time-top-five entries (Parenthood S1, Touched by an Angel S5, Are You Afraid of the Dark? 100S3) sit on vote shares below 3% and would be invisible if you weighted by audience size.

101None of this overturns the headline. 102It re-shapes it: more shows, denser top, with the prestige class growing faster than the average. 103Independent re-analyses extending past 2018 confirm the same trend, with a flattening only emerging around 2022–2023, after this dataset ends. 104That is what people mean when they say we are living through a golden age of television — and the data, at least through 2018, says they are right.
