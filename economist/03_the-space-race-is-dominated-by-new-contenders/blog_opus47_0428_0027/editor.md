# A new space race

## Story Spine
**Core claim**: The two-superpower duopoly that defined the space age is gone — in 2018 China launched more rockets than anyone else, and nearly half of all orbital launches were flown by private firms. The question is no longer whether the United States and Russia run space; it is who is going to.

**Tension**: Most readers still picture the space race as a Cold-War story between the US and the USSR/Russia. The headline numbers say that picture is sixty years out of date.

**Payoff**: Reframes "space" from nostalgic Cold-War nostalgia to live 21st-century industrial geopolitics — China is the busiest launcher; SpaceX alone matches the output of mid-sized national programmes; reaching orbit has gone from a coin-flip in the 1950s to a routine industrial process today.

## Sections

### edt_01: The new leader on the launchpad
**Evidence**: ana_06, ana_04 | **Context**: det_04

[ana_06] In 2018, for the first time in space-age history, China is the world's busiest launcher of orbital rockets. Through October it has flown 28 successful launches — one more than the United States (27) and three times as many as Russia (9). France/Europe trails at 6, Japan at 5, India at 4.

[ana_04, det_04] That sentence would have been unthinkable for almost the entire space age. China launched its first satellite, Dongfanghong-1, in 1970 and reached orbit twice in the entire 1970s. As recently as 2005 it flew just five times. Then the curve bent: 15 launches in 2010, 19 by 2012, 28 in 2018.

[editorial] The graphic-detail piece this story is built on appeared in The Economist on 18 October 2018 — and it captured the precise moment a transition the world had been talking about for years finally showed up in the data.

### edt_02: How we got here — the original duopoly
**Evidence**: ana_03, ana_02 | **Context**: det_01, det_03

[ana_02, det_01] The space age began on 4 October 1957 with Sputnik. Sixty-one years and 5,726 attempted orbital launches later, the cumulative ledger still bears the imprint of two superpowers. The single busiest year on record is 1967 with 139 launch attempts — peak Apollo on the American side, peak Zenit reconnaissance on the Soviet side.

[ana_03] Across the entire 1957–2018 record, the USSR and Russian Federation combined account for 56.2% of every successful orbital launch ever flown — 3,024 of 5,384. The United States is second with 29.4% (1,585). Together those two states account for 86% of every successful flight to orbit in human history.

[det_03] For the first three decades of the space age this was effectively the entire industry. China managed two orbital launches in the entire 1970s. France's whole programme through 1985 added up to fewer flights than the Soviets ran in a typical month.

### edt_03: Then the Soviet machine fell off a cliff
**Evidence**: ana_05, ana_04 | **Context**: det_03

[ana_05] The Soviet Union's busiest year was 1982, with 101 successful orbital launches. The cadence held above 90 per year through the late 1980s. Then the country dissolved. By the late 1990s Russia was averaging 25.2 launches a year — a 4.0× drop from peak.

[editorial] No other major space programme has ever lost three-quarters of its capacity in a decade. The fall reset the world's launch infrastructure overnight: a planet that had grown used to two state-scale rocket fleets suddenly had one and a half.

[ana_04] Russia never recovered the Soviet cadence. Its 2018 total — 9 successful launches — is its lowest since the early 1960s.

### edt_04: The third pole — China climbs the ladder
**Evidence**: ana_11, ana_12 | **Context**: det_04

[ana_12] The torch-pass is visible in the decadal totals. In the 1980s the Soviet Union flew 931 launches against 153 from the US — a Soviet decade. In the 1990s the US (283) overtook Russia (411) on slope as Moscow collapsed. By the 2010s the three big launchers are nearly tied: US 187, Russia 199, China 167.

[ana_11] In cumulative terms China is still distant — 289 successful launches all-time versus 1,585 for the US and 3,024 for Russia. But China added more launches in the 2010s than it had flown in the previous five decades combined. The slope is what counts.

[editorial] The arithmetic is straightforward: hold the current rates and China passes the United States cumulatively in the 2030s. Whether that actually happens depends on what the United States — and SpaceX — do next.

### edt_05: The other transformation — private rockets
**Evidence**: ana_14, ana_07, ana_10 | **Context**: det_05

[ana_14] For the first 23 years of the space age, every single orbital launch was flown by a state agency. The first private launch in this dataset is in 1980. By 1995 a hair under half (43.5%) of launches were commercial. In the 2015–18 window the share is 49.4% — and 15.2% of all launches are flown by what the Economist calls 'startups' (Rocket Lab, Sea Launch, Land Launch, and others), a category that did not exist a decade earlier.

[ana_10, det_05] One firm dominates the new private layer. SpaceX flew its first successful orbital launch in 2008 (Falcon 1, Flight 4). Through 2013 it averaged about two launches a year. Then: 6, 6, 8, 18, 17. By 2017 SpaceX alone was flying as much as the entire mid-2000s commercial industry.

[ana_07] Tally up 2018: 19 private launches plus 18 startup launches makes 37 — about 47% of the world's orbital launches in a year that also produced China's overtake. Two structural transitions, one calendar year.

### edt_06: A maturing industry — failure rates collapse
**Evidence**: ana_09 | **Context**: det_06

[ana_09] One number captures how far the industry has come. In the 1950s, 53.85% of all orbital launch attempts failed. The 1960s rate was 13.0%. By the 1980s it was 2.94%, the lowest decadal rate ever recorded — Soyuz, Proton, and the Space Shuttle were all running mature production lines. The 2010s rate is 3.7%.

[det_06] Reaching orbit has gone from roughly a coin-flip-with-extra-loss in the late 1950s to a routine industrial process by 2018. That is the precondition for everything else: GPS, weather satellites, satellite TV, broadband from low-Earth orbit, Earth-observation imagery — every application that depends on putting hardware reliably above the atmosphere.

### edt_07: The rocket that did most of the work
**Evidence**: ana_13, ana_08 | **Context**: det_03

[ana_13] One detail buried in the data: the most-flown orbital rocket in history is the Soviet Soyuz-U, with 632 flights. Eight of the top ten most-flown launchers in the world are Russian. NASA's Space Shuttle, often treated as the iconic launch vehicle of the era, ranks sixth at 135 flights — fewer than the Voskhod 11A57 from the 1960s.

[ana_08] At the agency level the same shape holds. Of the top ten organisations by all-time launches, only one was founded in this century: SpaceX, ranked eighth, the only post-2002 entrant in the all-time top ten.

[editorial] That is what makes the current moment striking. The hardware that built the space age was Soviet, the operators were states, and the ratio held for half a century. In the span of a decade — through this dataset — both halves of that pattern broke at once.

### edt_08: What 2018 actually means
**Evidence**: ana_06, ana_07, ana_10 | **Context**: det_04, det_05, det_08

[editorial] The point of the 2018 data is not the rivalry leaderboard. It is what the leaderboard implies: orbit is no longer the exclusive preserve of two Cold-War states, and not even the exclusive preserve of states.

[det_08] Orbit is now infrastructure. GPS, weather forecasts, broadband, climate observation, military reconnaissance — all of it depends on satellites that need to be put up there reliably and cheaply. The shift from two-superpower duopoly to multi-polar mix is therefore not a niche aerospace story; it is a story about who controls the foundational layer of 21st-century communications and surveillance.

[ana_06, ana_10] In the year The Economist took its snapshot, China launched the most rockets, SpaceX launched as much as a small national programme, and the new contenders together flew nearly half of every flight to orbit. That is a different industry than the one Sputnik started.
