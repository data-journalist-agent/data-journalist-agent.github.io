016The new leader on the launchpad

7In 2018, for the first time in space-age history, China is the world's busiest launcher of orbital rockets. 8Through October it has flown 28 successful launches — one more than the United States (27) and three times as many as Russia (9). 9France/Europe trails at 6, Japan at 5, India at 4.

10That sentence would have been unthinkable for almost the entire space age. 11China launched its first satellite, Dongfanghong-1, in 1970 and reached orbit twice in the entire 1970s. 12As recently as 2005 it flew just five times. 13Then the curve bent: 15 launches in 2010, 19 by 2012, 28 in 2018.

14The graphic-detail piece this story is built on appeared in The Economist on 18 October 2018 — and it captured the precise moment a transition the world had been talking about for years finally showed up in the data.

0220How we got here — the original duopoly

21The space age began on 4 October 1957 with Sputnik. 22Sixty-one years and 5,726 attempted orbital launches later, the cumulative ledger still bears the imprint of two superpowers. 23The single busiest year on record is 1967 with 139 launch attempts — peak Apollo on the American side, peak Zenit reconnaissance on the Soviet side.

24Across the entire 1957–2018 record, the USSR and Russian Federation combined account for 56.2% of every successful orbital launch ever flown — 3,024 of 5,384. 25The United States is second with 29.4% (1,585). 26Together those two states account for 86% of every successful flight to orbit in human history.

27For the first three decades of the space age this was effectively the entire industry. 28China managed two orbital launches in the entire 1970s. 29France's whole programme through 1985 added up to fewer flights than the Soviets ran in a typical month.

0332Then the Soviet machine fell off a cliff

33The Soviet Union's busiest year was 1982, with 101 successful orbital launches. 34The cadence held above 90 per year through the late 1980s. 35Then the country dissolved. 36By the late 1990s Russia was averaging 25.2 launches a year — a 4.0× drop from peak.

37No other major space programme has ever lost three-quarters of its capacity in a decade. 38The fall reset the world's launch infrastructure overnight: a planet that had grown used to two state-scale rocket fleets suddenly had one and a half.

39Russia never recovered the Soviet cadence. 40Its 2018 total — 9 successful launches — is its lowest since the early 1960s.

0445The third pole — China climbs the ladder

46The torch-pass is visible in the decadal totals. 47In the 1980s the Soviet Union flew 931 launches against 153 from the US — a Soviet decade. 48In the 1990s the US (283) overtook Russia (411) on slope as Moscow collapsed. 49By the 2010s the three big launchers are nearly tied: US 187, Russia 199, China 167.

50In cumulative terms China is still distant — 289 successful launches all-time versus 1,585 for the US and 3,024 for Russia. 51But China added more launches in the 2010s than it had flown in the previous five decades combined. 52The slope is what counts.

53The arithmetic is straightforward: hold the current rates and China passes the United States cumulatively in the 2030s. 54Whether that actually happens depends on what the United States — and SpaceX — do next.

0560The other transformation — private rockets

61For the first 23 years of the space age, every single orbital launch was flown by a state agency. 62The first private launch in this dataset is in 1980. 63By 1995 a hair under half (43.5%) of launches were commercial. 64In the 2015–18 window the share is 49.4% — and 15.2% of all launches are flown by what the Economist calls 'startups' (Rocket Lab, Sea Launch, Land Launch, and others), a category that did not exist a decade earlier.

65One firm dominates the new private layer. 66SpaceX flew its first successful orbital launch in 2008 (Falcon 1, Flight 4). 67Through 2013 it averaged about two launches a year. 68Then: 6, 6, 8, 18, 17. 69By 2017 SpaceX alone was flying as much as the entire mid-2000s commercial industry.

70Tally up 2018: 19 private launches plus 18 startup launches makes 37 — about 47% of the world's orbital launches in a year that also produced China's overtake. 71Two structural transitions, one calendar year.

0678A maturing industry — failure rates collapse

79One number captures how far the industry has come. 80In the 1950s, 53.85% of all orbital launch attempts failed. 81The 1960s rate was 13.0%. 82By the 1980s it was 2.94%, the lowest decadal rate ever recorded — Soyuz, Proton, and the Space Shuttle were all running mature production lines. 83The 2010s rate is 3.7%.

84Reaching orbit has gone from roughly a coin-flip-with-extra-loss in the late 1950s to a routine industrial process by 2018. 85That is the precondition for everything else: GPS, weather satellites, satellite TV, broadband from low-Earth orbit, Earth-observation imagery — every application that depends on putting hardware reliably above the atmosphere.

0788The rocket that did most of the work

89One detail buried in the data: the most-flown orbital rocket in history is the Soviet Soyuz-U, with 632 flights. 90Eight of the top ten most-flown launchers in the world are Russian. 91NASA's Space Shuttle, often treated as the iconic launch vehicle of the era, ranks sixth at 135 flights — fewer than the Voskhod 11A57 from the 1960s.

92At the agency level the same shape holds. 93Of the top ten organisations by all-time launches, only one was founded in this century: SpaceX, ranked eighth, the only post-2002 entrant in the all-time top ten.

94That is what makes the current moment striking. 95The hardware that built the space age was Soviet, the operators were states, and the ratio held for half a century. 96In the span of a decade — through this dataset — both halves of that pattern broke at once.

0899What 2018 actually means

100The point of the 2018 data is not the rivalry leaderboard. 101It is what the leaderboard implies: orbit is no longer the exclusive preserve of two Cold-War states, and not even the exclusive preserve of states.

102Orbit is now infrastructure. 103GPS, weather forecasts, broadband, climate observation, military reconnaissance — all of it depends on satellites that need to be put up there reliably and cheaply. 104The shift from two-superpower duopoly to multi-polar mix is therefore not a niche aerospace story; it is a story about who controls the foundational layer of 21st-century communications and surveillance.

105In the year The Economist took its snapshot, China launched the most rockets, SpaceX launched as much as a small national programme, and the new contenders together flew nearly half of every flight to orbit. 106That is a different industry than the one Sputnik started.
