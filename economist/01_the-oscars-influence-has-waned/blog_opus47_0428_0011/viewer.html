2The Most-Referenced Film of 1980 Was Not Nominated

3For 89 years, Best Picture has crowned the films the Academy thinks will last. 4The films audiences actually quote, parody and remake are increasingly chosen by it not 5to receive Best Picture.

6In 1980, the most-referenced film of the year — by a factor of 18.6 — was 7The Empire Strikes Back. 8The Academy gave Best Picture to Ordinary People. 9The same year-#1 film, never even nominated, repeats again and again: Psycho 10(1960), 112001: A Space Odyssey 12(1968), The Terminator 13(1984), Back to the Future 14(1985), Jurassic Park 15(1993), The Matrix 16(1999), Pulp Fiction lost to Forrest Gump, Goodfellas lost to Dances with Wolves.

17Of the 89 years from 1928 to 2016, the most-referenced film was not nominated for Best Picture in 52 of them — 58.4% of all years. Star Wars, Empire, Psycho, Frankenstein, Snow White, 2001, King Kong18: the Academy did not consider any of them the year's best.

26How a Reference Is Counted

27Influence here is operationalized as IMDb "movie connections" — every time a later film, TV show, video game or music video quotes, parodies, homages or references the original. When Niles in Frasier says 28"Of all the coffee joints in all the towns in all the world…"29, that is one connection added to Casablanca. 30The Psycho strings in any horror comedy are connections to Psycho. 31The data is crowdsourced from millions of viewers.

The Economist 38assembled the data in early 2019 from a frozen IMDb snapshot taken at the end of 2017. 39They counted 28,763 films, computed each film's share of references among films released in the same year, and tagged Best Picture nominees and winners. 40We use the same numbers here, capped at 2016 releases as they did.

41The obvious caveat is the recency problem: an older film has had more decades to accrue references, so we do not compare counts directly across years. 42Within a single year, every film is competing on the same clock — and that is the comparison the Academy itself is making when it picks Best Picture.

43The Academy's Hit Rate Is Collapsing

44The clearest test of the Academy's instinct is whether the winner is the year-#1 most-referenced film. 45Out of 89 winners, 14 — just 15.7% — were also #1 in their own year. 46The Academy is right less than one year in six.

47The decade-by-decade trend is starker. 48In the 1960s and 1970s, 30% of winners were the year-#1 film. 49In the 1980s the rate fell to zero. 50It bounced back to 20% in the 1990s — then collapsed again. 51Across all seven Best Picture awards from 2010 to 2016, the year-#1 film was never the winner. Not once.

52The mean year-rank of Best Picture winners follows the same curve: 3.5 in the 1970s, 14.0 in the 1980s, 11.6 in the 2010s. 53The Academy's eye for the era-defining film, sharpest in the New Hollywood years, has not recovered.

58Best Picture, Year by Year

59The path of every Best Picture winner through the rankings is a reverse trajectory. Casablanca 60(1943) captured 48.7% of all references for its year. The Godfather 61(1972) took 33.7%. Titanic 62(1997) took 17.2%. Spotlight 63(2015) — the most recent winner before our cutoff — took 0.78%. Moonlight 64(2016) took 1.08%.

65The most recent winner above 10% was Titanic. 66The most recent winner above 5% was 2003's 67Lord of the Rings: The Return of the King. 68The current decade hovers between 1% and 2.5%.

95The Films the Academy Chooses Now

96Twenty-two Best Picture winners — almost a quarter — finished outside the top 10 most-referenced films of their own year. 97The two most extreme: Cavalcade 98(1933, ranked 37th in its year) and The Last Emperor 99(1987, ranked 36th). 100In the 2010s, Spotlight 101ranked 25th and Moonlight 10215th. Argo 103finished 11th. Only Birdman 104(6th) cracked the top 10 for the entire decade.

105There is a pattern in what the Academy chooses now: intimate, sober dramas. Spotlight, Moonlight, The Artist, Birdman, 10612 Years a Slave. 107These are reasonable films. 108But they generate few visual or verbal hooks for the wider culture to grab. 109Genre cinema — sci-fi, horror, action — is parody-rich, quote-rich, image-rich. 110The Academy votes against it almost reflexively.

114The Cinema-Shaped Hole Is Smaller, Too

115One reading of the decline is that audiences and creators are simply more spread out. 116The single most-referenced film of each year used to dominate. 117In the 1970s the year-#1 captured 22.6% of all references on average. 118In the 2000s it fell to 7.2%. 119In the 2010s, just 5.7% — a 4x drop from the 1970s peak. Even 120Star Wars: The Force Awakens121, the most-referenced film of 2015, took only 9.0% of that year's references.

122So the Academy's failure is not just relative — the cinema-shaped hole in the culture is bigger than it used to be. 123But the Academy is failing within that smaller hole. When The Force Awakens 124captured 9% and Spotlight 125captured 0.78%, the Academy chose a film that was 11 times less referenced than the year's leader.

129What Is Left of the Canon

130The Oscars used to function as a canon-maker. 131Winners got re-released, taught in film schools, shelved in the front of video stores. 132Best Picture viewership peaked at 55 million in 1998 — Titanic133's year — and has been falling ever since. 134By 2018 it was 26.5 million, less than half.

135None of this means the Academy is wrong about the films it does pick. Moonlight and Spotlight are good films. 136The point is narrower: the Academy used to be a reliable predictor of what the world would still talk about. It is not, anymore. 137The films we still quote, parody, and remake are increasingly chosen by the Academy not 138to receive Best Picture.

139On the night a Best Picture winner is announced, the safest bet is that fewer people will reference it twenty years from now than will reference at least three other films released in the same year. 140That has been true, on average, for every decade since the 1980s. 141It will likely be true again next year.
