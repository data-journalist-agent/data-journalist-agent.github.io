## Story Spine
**Core claim**: Best Picture used to crown the films that defined their year. It barely does any more — the Academy now reliably picks films the world will not remember.
**Tension**: The Oscars are sold as cultural canonization. The data show that for two of the last four decades, the winner has not even been the most-referenced film of its own year — and 22 winners are ranked outside the top 10.
**Payoff**: When you look at the films your friends actually quote, parody and rewatch, you are watching the Academy choose around them. The decline is decade by decade, measurable, and it is accelerating.

## Sections

### edt_01: Hook — The most-referenced film of 1980 was not nominated
**Evidence**: ana_06, ana_16 | **Context**: det_07, det_09

[ana_06] In 1980, the most-referenced film of the year — by a factor of 18.6 — was *The Empire Strikes Back*. The Academy gave Best Picture to *Ordinary People*. The same year-#1 film, never even nominated, repeats again and again: *Psycho* (1960), *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968), *The Terminator* (1984), *Back to the Future* (1985), *Jurassic Park* (1993), *The Matrix* (1999), *Pulp Fiction* lost to *Forrest Gump*, *Goodfellas* lost to *Dances with Wolves*.

[ana_06] Of the 89 years from 1928 to 2016, the most-referenced film was not nominated for Best Picture in 52 of them — 58.4% of all years. Star Wars, *Empire*, *Psycho*, *Frankenstein*, *Snow White*, *2001*, *King Kong*: the Academy did not consider any of them the year's best.

### edt_02: How we measure influence
**Evidence**: | **Context**: det_01, det_02, det_03

[det_02] Influence here is operationalized as IMDb "movie connections" — every time a later film, TV show, video game or music video quotes, parodies, homages or references the original. When Niles in *Frasier* says *"Of all the coffee joints in all the towns in all the world…"*, that is one connection added to *Casablanca*. The Psycho strings in any horror comedy are connections to *Psycho*. The data is crowdsourced from millions of viewers.

[det_01] *The Economist* assembled the data in early 2019 from a frozen IMDb snapshot taken at the end of 2017. They counted 28,763 films, computed each film's share of references among films released in the same year, and tagged Best Picture nominees and winners. We use the same numbers here, capped at 2016 releases as they did.

[det_03] The obvious caveat is the recency problem: an older film has had more decades to accrue references, so we do not compare counts directly across years. Within a single year, every film is competing on the same clock — and that is the comparison the Academy itself is making when it picks Best Picture.

### edt_03: The Academy's hit rate is collapsing
**Evidence**: ana_12, ana_04 | **Context**: det_05, det_06

[ana_12] The clearest test of the Academy's instinct is whether the winner is the year-#1 most-referenced film. Out of 89 winners, 14 — just 15.7% — were also #1 in their own year. The Academy is right less than one year in six.

[ana_12, ana_04] The decade-by-decade trend is starker. In the 1960s and 1970s, 30% of winners were the year-#1 film. In the 1980s the rate fell to zero. It bounced back to 20% in the 1990s — then collapsed again. Across all seven Best Picture awards from 2010 to 2016, the year-#1 film was never the winner. Not once.

[ana_04] The mean year-rank of Best Picture winners follows the same curve: 3.5 in the 1970s, 14.0 in the 1980s, 11.6 in the 2010s. The Academy's eye for the era-defining film, sharpest in the New Hollywood years, has not recovered.

### edt_04: Best Picture, year by year
**Evidence**: ana_07, ana_10 | **Context**: det_05

[ana_07] The path of every Best Picture winner through the rankings is a reverse trajectory. *Casablanca* (1943) captured 48.7% of all references for its year. *The Godfather* (1972) took 33.7%. *Titanic* (1997) took 17.2%. *Spotlight* (2015) — the most recent winner before our cutoff — took 0.78%. *Moonlight* (2016) took 1.08%.

[ana_07] The most recent winner above 10% was *Titanic*. The most recent winner above 5% was 2003's *Lord of the Rings: The Return of the King*. The current decade hovers between 1% and 2.5%.

[ana_10] Set the winners aside and rebuild the record from the films that audiences actually reference. The most-referenced film of each year is a parallel canon: *Frankenstein* (1931), *King Kong* (1933), *Snow White* (1937), *The Wizard of Oz* (1939), *Citizen Kane* (1941), *Vertigo* (1958), *Psycho* (1960), *2001* (1968), *The Godfather* (1972), *Jaws* (1975), *Star Wars* (1977), *Pulp Fiction* (1994), *Titanic* (1997), *The Matrix* (1999). Sixteen of these are not Best Picture winners. Many were never nominated.

### edt_05: The kind of film the Academy chooses
**Evidence**: ana_05, ana_08 | **Context**: det_07

[ana_05] Twenty-two Best Picture winners — almost a quarter — finished outside the top 10 most-referenced films of their own year. The two most extreme: *Cavalcade* (1933, ranked 37th in its year) and *The Last Emperor* (1987, ranked 36th). In the 2010s, *Spotlight* ranked 25th and *Moonlight* 15th. *Argo* finished 11th. Only *Birdman* (6th) cracked the top 10 for the entire decade.

[ana_08, det_07] There is a pattern in what the Academy chooses now: intimate, sober dramas. *Spotlight*, *Moonlight*, *The Artist*, *Birdman*, *12 Years a Slave*. These are reasonable films. But they generate few visual or verbal hooks for the wider culture to grab. Genre cinema — sci-fi, horror, action — is parody-rich, quote-rich, image-rich. The Academy votes against it almost reflexively.

### edt_06: Even the top film commands less of the year
**Evidence**: ana_09 | **Context**: det_03

[ana_09] One reading of the decline is that audiences and creators are simply more spread out. The single most-referenced film of each year used to dominate. In the 1970s the year-#1 captured 22.6% of all references on average. In the 2000s it fell to 7.2%. In the 2010s, just 5.7% — a 4x drop from the 1970s peak. Even *Star Wars: The Force Awakens*, the most-referenced film of 2015, took only 9.0% of that year's references.

[ana_09, det_03] So the Academy's failure is not just relative — the cinema-shaped hole in the culture is bigger than it used to be. But the Academy is failing within that smaller hole. When *The Force Awakens* captured 9% and *Spotlight* captured 0.78%, the Academy chose a film that was 11 times less referenced than the year's leader.

### edt_07: What is left of the canonization machine
**Evidence**: | **Context**: det_06, det_07

[det_06] The Oscars used to function as a canon-maker. Winners got re-released, taught in film schools, shelved in the front of video stores. Best Picture viewership peaked at 55 million in 1998 — *Titanic*'s year — and has been falling ever since. By 2018 it was 26.5 million, less than half.

[editorial] None of this means the Academy is wrong about the films it does pick. *Moonlight* and *Spotlight* are good films. The point is narrower: the Academy used to be a reliable predictor of what the world would still talk about. It is not, anymore. The films we still quote, parody, and remake are increasingly chosen by the Academy *not* to receive Best Picture.

[editorial] On the night a Best Picture winner is announced, the safest bet is that fewer people will reference it twenty years from now than will reference at least three other films released in the same year. That has been true, on average, for every decade since the 1980s. It will likely be true again next year.
