5A reanalysis of mobility, stringency, and six decades of epidemics · Based on data and replication code published with The Economist's Graphic Detail piece, 6 June 2020

6In April 2020 the citizens of democracies stayed away from shops, restaurants and cinemas at a rate of 75.6% below normal. 7The citizens of autocracies — operating, by reputation, under stricter and faster orders — stayed away at only 61.9% below normal. 8A gap of 13.7 percentage points, in the single category of activity most clearly tied to voluntary behaviour rather than state coercion.

9The same pattern shows up everywhere Google was tracking. 10At workplaces democracies fell 14.7 points further; at parks 13.4 points; at grocery and pharmacy 8.1 points. 11The only place autocracies kept pace was transit stations, where the gap shrinks to 4.2 points — plausibly because shutting trains is something a state can do directly, without asking anyone to comply.

12In the months before this data arrived, the dominant question in opinion pages was whether democracies could even mobilise the same societal effort as autocracies. 13By April, the answer had quietly turned out to be: not just yes, but more.

21The gap was not a one-week artefact. 22Across all five mobility categories, democracies cut movement by 13.0 points more than autocracies in March, 10.8 points more in April, and 11.0 points more in May. 23Both groups eased in late spring, but neither closed the gap.

24Google's Community Mobility Reports — released in April 2020, drawing on aggregated location histories from Android phones — measure each day against a five-week baseline at the start of the year. 25The report covers six categories of place; the Economist analysed five (omitting "residential", which is mostly the inverse of the others). 26The full picture, month by month, category by category, is a near-monotonic story: democracies below autocracies, every cell.

34Stricter laws are not the answer

35You might expect the explanation to be straightforward: democracies wrote stricter laws, so their citizens moved less. 36Two facts make that story collapse.

37The first: at the country level, democracies and autocracies wrote almost identical lockdown laws. 38Across 110 countries with full April 2020 data — 74 democracies, 36 non-democracies — the average Stringency Index was 79.3 in democracies and 80.6 in autocracies. 39On paper, democracies were less strict. 40In practice they had a workplace mobility drop of -48.1%, against -38.9% in autocracies — a 9.2-point gap moving the opposite direction to what the laws would predict.

57Every country, two numbers

58Plot every country with full data and the cloud separates. 59Democracies cluster in the corner of high stringency and large mobility drops — Argentina (-53.1%), Bolivia (-74.7%), Ecuador (-74.9%), Spain (-68.6%), Italy (-63.9%). 60Autocracies with similar laws tend to land short: Egypt at -36.5%, Russia at -41.5%, Uganda at -27.6% despite a posted Stringency Index of 89.4.

61The most interesting outlier is in the democracy 62set: Japan, with a stringency of 45.7 — well below the global average — yet a 21.9% drop in workplace mobility. 63Voluntary distancing, achieved without strict orders. 64Sweden too, at the low end of stringency (46.3) and the low end of mobility drop (-19.6%), arrived at a different equilibrium for different reasons.

71Sixty years of epidemics tell the same story

72The COVID-era picture isn't an accident of one virus. 73EM-DAT — the international disaster database run by CRED at UCLouvain, with criteria of ten or more deaths or a declared emergency — has logged 1,062 epidemics worldwide since 1960 in countries we can classify. 74Democracies experienced 394 of these and lost a median 34 people per outbreak. 75Autocracies experienced 668 and lost a median 45.5. 76Even with autocracies' notorious under-reporting biasing the comparison the other 77way, non-democracies still come out worse.

78The historical pattern flipped sometime in the late twentieth century. 79In the 1980s — the era of military dictatorships in Latin America and one-party states in Africa — the median was actually higher in democracies, driven by particular outbreaks in India and Brazil. 80From the 1990s onward the picture inverts and stays inverted: in every decade since, autocracies have the higher median. 81The 2010s sit at 43 deaths per outbreak in autocracies, 27 in democracies.

87Compliance, not coercion

88The temptation in spring 2020 was to read the pandemic as a test of state capacity, where authoritarianism would win. 89The result was that the test ran on something else: how much trust there was between citizen and state, and how much voluntary co-operation a government could call on.

90When the rules are roughly the same, the system that converts laws into behaviour better — wins. 91That, very awkwardly for the original framing, turns out to be the system where citizens get to argue with the rules in the first place.

92None of this means democracies handled the pandemic well in absolute terms. 93Italy and the United States and the UK had catastrophic spring waves; the variation within 94each regime type was as wide as the gap between them. 95The argument is comparative, not exonerating.

96What it does mean is that the question for the next pandemic isn't "should the rules be tougher?" — it's "will anyone listen?".
