2The manager dial is tiny

3A re-analysis of 15 seasons of Europe's big-five leagues finds that one Lionel Messi outweighs more than two of the best managers — and Carlo Ancelotti ranks among the worst 10 of 596 currently-employed coaches.

4Based on data published by The Economist · 19 January 2019

5One Messi, two and a half Favres

6A bar chart that ends an argument

7Cristiano Ronaldo and Lionel Messi each rate +9.24 points per season above an average peer in their position. 8The best-projected manager in football — Lucien Favre, then at Borussia Dortmund — rates +3.76. 9One Messi, the model says, is worth roughly two and a half Lucien Favres.

10That ratio reframes a thing fans love to argue about. 11Football culture treats managers as decisive figures: pundits credit promoted teams' survival to a "manager bounce", clubs sack coaches mid-season hoping for an instant lift. 12Yet across 596 currently employed top-flight managers, the gap between the best and worst projected impact is only 6.56 points per season — a span any single elite player exceeds on his own.

16How the model knows what to expect

17Forecasting football with a video game

18To separate manager effects from squad quality, The Economist used FIFA's video-game ratings, scored 0–99 each season by EA's analysts and a network of about 9,000 dedicated scouts. 19Those ratings were available before each season started, which is the trick: they measure player skill independent of that year's results.

20Player ratings were z-scored within position groups and exponentiated, then summed for each squad's first-choice goalkeeper, top five defenders, and top seven attackers. 21The resulting forecasts come out within 7.27 points (mean absolute error) of actual league points, across 1,470 team-seasons. 22Three quarters of team-seasons land within 10 points; almost half land within 5. 23That accuracy — better than betting markets in some seasons — is what makes the rest of this analysis honest.

2612 times the dial

27Squad spread vs manager spread

28Across 1,470 team-seasons in five leagues, the gap between the strongest squad's expected points (96.3) and the weakest (15.5) is 80.8 points. 29Across 596 currently-employed managers, the gap between best and worst projected impact is 6.56 points. 30Squad differences are roughly twelve times larger than manager differences.

31Put another way, the league table is overwhelmingly written by the cheque book. 32The manager fine-tunes within an envelope someone else has already drawn.

37The household-name table

38Sarri above Pep, Ancelotti at the bottom

39Apply the projection to football's household names and the order is humbling. 40Maurizio Sarri (+3.04) edges Jürgen Klopp (+2.85), and both sit above Pep Guardiola (+1.85) and José Mourinho (+0.84). 41Sir Alex Ferguson, projected from a partial Premier League record, lands at +2.02. 42Arsène Wenger comes out at -0.35; Zinedine Zidane at -0.63; Marcelo Bielsa at -1.01.

43The single most striking name in the bottom ten is Carlo Ancelotti, then at Napoli, at -2.80 — second-worst of all 596 currently employed coaches. 44Ancelotti has won league titles in four countries and the Champions League three times. 45The model says the league points he produced track what his squads were already projected to produce, and at recent stops he has been below the line.

46None of this means Ancelotti is a bad coach or that Pep is overrated. 47It means the gap between revered and merely competent is a fraction of what fans assume. 48Most of the prestige is built on running excellent squads.

59Past success, future uncertainty

60Barely better than a coin flip

61Among 222 managers with at least two tenures of 15+ league games, only 45% of those who beat expectations in their first job did so again in the next one. 62The Economist's article reports 51%; the published data here gives 45%. 63Either way the message is the same: past overperformance is barely better than a coin flip.

9 45% 64of overachieving managers do it again in their next job — barely better than a coin flip.

65To respect that uncertainty, the model adds 461 games of league-average performance to every manager's record before projecting his future. 66A coach with one season's worth of data carries 7.6% of his record forward; with five seasons, 29.2%; only after a full decade does he carry close to half. 67The Premier League sacks managers faster than this curve catches up.

71When the model breaks

72Leicester up, Mourinho's Chelsea down

73The dataset's biggest single-season surprise is Leicester's 2015–16 Premier League title: a squad projected to take 40.2 points won 81. 74RB Leipzig's first Bundesliga season (2016–17) ran 31.3 points hot. 75Montpellier won Ligue 1 in 2011–12 with 31.2 points more than expected. 76These are exactly the seasons football culture remembers as managerial fairy tales.

77On the other side: Mourinho's defending-champion Chelsea collapsed by 31.4 points in 2015–16, sacking him in December. 782010 Juventus and 2013 Inter both undershot by more than 23. 79When pundits demand a manager's head after a bad run, this is the data they are pointing at.

80But the model attributes most of these gaps to noise — random variance that does not repeat. 81Of the 1,220 manager-team tenures in the big-five leagues since 2004, two-thirds end before reaching one full season. 82We fire managers on samples too small to mean anything, then attribute the next bounce — usually regression to the mean — to the new face on the touchline.

85What the dial actually does

86If you want to know who will win, look at the wage bill

87Even within the small space managers occupy, where you spend matters. 88The top attackers in this data add nine points a season; the top goalkeepers add a fifth of one. 89The 95th-percentile attacker (+3.85) is alone worth more than the best projected manager. 90Buying the right striker is, on this evidence, a far bigger lever than hiring the right boss.

93So why do clubs spend so much energy on the touchline? 94Because firing a manager is a thing you can do this week. 95Squad quality is a multi-year project; coaches are the visible decision. 96The Economist's analysis doesn't say managers are useless — Favre, Simeone, Klopp clearly squeeze more out of their squads than peers — only that the dial they turn is small, and that most decisions made in their name are reading luck as skill.

97If you want to know who will win next season, look at the wage bill. 98If you want to know who got fired, follow the variance.
