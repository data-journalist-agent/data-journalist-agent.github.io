The Data

The NBA's Homework

2For three and a half seasons, the league has published a PDF every morning saying exactly which late-game referee decisions were wrong. 3Twenty-five thousand calls later, the folklore about NBA officiating mostly isn't supported by the league's own receipts.

401 · Origin

5The first time the NBA said "we were wrong"

6On the morning of March 2, 2015, the NBA did something no American pro sports league had done before. 7It published a PDF listing every late-game referee decision from the night before, labeled whether each was right or wrong, with the officials' names attached. 8The program was called the Last Two Minute Report. 9It arrived eight years after referee Tim Donaghy went to federal prison for betting on games he officiated, and three years before the Supreme Court legalized sports gambling nationwide.

10Since then, the league has graded 25,057 of its own late-game calls across 1,473 games. 11Of those, it said it got 91.23% right — a blend of 29.58% correct calls, 61.65% correct non-calls, 0.96% wrongful whistles, and 7.81% missed whistles.

12That last number is the one that actually tells the story. 13The NBA's referee mistakes are overwhelmingly not whistles that shouldn't have blown. 14They're whistles that should have and didn't.

2102 · Asymmetry

22When the refs get it wrong, they get it wrong by silence

23Of the 2,197 mistakes the league admits to, 1,957 were missed calls and only 240 were wrongful calls. 24That's an 8.2-to-1 ratio. 25When an NBA referee gets it wrong in crunch time, they almost always get it wrong by doing nothing.

26The missed calls pile up most heavily on shooting fouls (382) and offensive fouls (349), but the biggest per-play failure rate is somewhere else entirely: defensive three-second violations are missed 63.6% of the time, and traveling violations are missed 43.9% of the time. 27These are the violations that require constant attention during live action, not moment-of-contact judgment. 28The crew is good at judging what they see. 29They're bad at noticing things they're not already watching.

3303 · Per-game budget

34One admitted mistake per game

35A typical close NBA game ends with one or two calls the league will later admit were wrong. 36The average across 1,473 games is 1.49 errors per game. 3728.9% of games are clean — zero errors the league will flag. 38Another 29% have exactly one. 39But 3.6% (53 games) have five or more errors, and the single worst-officiated game in L2M history had fourteen.

40That's the right frame: not "the refs are bad" but "the refs budget one admitted mistake per close game, and a handful of nights a year they completely lose the plot."

4304 · Trend

44The league is getting better — fast

45In the program's first partial season (March–June 2015), the error rate was 13.03% — about one admitted mistake every 46 seconds of reviewed game time. 46Three years later in 2017-18 it was 6.15%, less than half. 47Errors per game fell from 1.84 to 1.27.

48The referees' union hates the L2M. 49They believe public scrutiny turns fans into coordinated pressure against officials. 50But the data is consistent with a simpler interpretation: when you grade people's work publicly and they see the grades, they get better.

5505 · The test case

56The Scott Foster test

57If the L2M data means anything, Scott Foster should be the first thing it damns. 58Players have voted Foster the worst referee in the NBA in two anonymous surveys — The LA Times in 2016 and The Athletic in 2023 (n=108). 59His nickname is "the Extender," for his uncanny knack of officiating playoff games that trailing teams end up winning. 60He received 134 phone calls from Tim Donaghy during Donaghy's betting period, though federal investigators cleared him. 61Chris Paul has an openly personal feud with him.

62And here is what the public data actually says. 63Scott Foster worked 94 L2M games — the most in the league. 64He was a member of the crew for 1,599 graded decisions. 65His error rate was 9.82%. 66The league median for referees with 40+ games is 8.83%. 67Foster ranks 49th out of 59 eligible referees, where 1 is cleanest. He is below median. 68He is not an outlier.

69Ten referees have higher error rates than Foster. 70Tre Maddox (11.00%), Gary Zielinski (10.80%), and Tony Brown (10.67%) are all measurably worse — yet not a single jersey is being made about them.

71Where Foster does show up is in the worst games. 72He was a member of the crew on two of the three most-erroneous games in the entire L2M history — Knicks at Hawks in January 2017 (11 errors) and Nuggets at Pelicans in March 2015 (10 errors). 73Which is enough to keep a reputation alive, but not enough to make the numbers say what his critics want them to.

7506 · Narrow spread

76The refs are more similar than the lore says

77Among referees who worked at least 40 L2M games, the worst error rate is 11.00% (Tre Maddox) and the best is 6.58% (Dedric Taylor). 78That's a range of 4.42 percentage points — meaningful but not dramatic. 79The bottom-ten cleanest referees sit between 6.58% and 7.55%. 80The top-ten worst sit between 10.03% and 11.00%.

81In other words: the "bad refs vs good refs" narrative is roughly a four-and-a-half-percentage-point story — fewer than one extra error every 23 close games. 82That is the entire spread of the modern NBA officiating crew, as measured by the league itself.

8407 · A myth collapses

85The home-court bias that isn't

86Across every referee error in the dataset where the disadvantaged team is identifiable — 2,165 calls — the home team was hurt 1,080 times (49.88%) and the away team 1,085 times (50.12%). 87A five-call difference across three and a half years.

88Published analyses of L2M data have argued there is a small, statistically significant pro-home bias. 89A 2023 peer-reviewed paper in Nature Scientific Reports found one. 90The Pudding found one in 2017. 91But in the clean four-season window observed here, the split is as close to 50/50 as real-world data ever gets. 92On wrongful-whistles (IC), home teams are actually hurt slightly more often (52.89% vs 47.11%). 93The "the refs are in the pocket of the home team" trope survives despite the data, not because of it.

9508 · The real asymmetry

96The real asymmetry is positional, not personal

97The L2M isn't kind to everybody equally. 98The player most hurt by referee errors is Andrew Wiggins — 22 times disadvantaged by a bad call, 7 times the beneficiary, net -15. 99Damian Lillard is net -14. 100Isaiah Thomas is -13. 101Kemba Walker is -12. 102These are the guards and wings whose game is driving into late-game contact — the kind of play that produces the hardest calls to judge.

103And on the other end, the players who "benefit" from the L2M's math are all big men. 104Marcin Gortat leads at net -38 (committed-error minus disadvantaged-error): five times hurt, forty-three times the committer. 105Andre Drummond, Karl-Anthony Towns, and Draymond Green are all at -21. 106Hassan Whiteside is -19. 107Al Horford is -19. 108Superstar treatment isn't uniform. 109Star guards lose fifteen close-game whistles over a few seasons; star bigs commit forty. 110The famous LeBron James is almost exactly neutral across 477 appearances — a net of -5 in 25 disadvantaged vs 20 committed.

111This is the most honest answer to "do the refs favor stars." The data says: they favor whoever happens to not be standing near the basket committing the act the league most often notices — which is overwhelmingly the guy attacking with the ball, not the guy defending without it.

11409 · The buzzer

115The buzzer isn't where the mistakes happen

116The last 30 seconds of a close NBA game account for 9,363 reviewed decisions — 37% of the entire four-season dataset despite being only 25% of the two-minute window. 117Crunch time is dense: timeouts cluster, fouls stack, the ball changes hands repeatedly, and every possession gets parsed.

118And yet in the most-scrutinized ten seconds — 0 to 9 seconds left — the error rate drops to 6.62%, the lowest of any ten-second bucket. 119The single worst window is 40–49 seconds, where 10.07% of decisions are graded wrong. 120The crew's hardest stretch is not at the end. 121It's a full 40 seconds before that.

122One way to read it: when the game truly hangs on a whistle, the league has built an ecosystem — video review, the replay center in Secaucus, multiple angles — that catches most of what would otherwise slip. 123The mistakes happen before the whole apparatus wakes up.

126What transparency actually changed

127The L2M launched in 2015 with a specific bet: that publishing mistakes, by name, would make the league more credible. 128Three years before PASPA fell and sports betting became legal, commissioner Adam Silver was already building the infrastructure of a league that could be trusted with billions in wagered dollars.

129The data says the bet paid off, at least on the narrow axis the program set out to measure. Error rates halved. 130Errors per game dropped by 31%. 131The referees' union wants the reports killed; the players, at least publicly, mostly agree. 132But in the four years of evidence we now have, the only group whose behavior changed visibly was the one being graded.

133The L2M did not make the refs perfect. 134It made them better. 135The next time a ref blows a whistle with three seconds on the clock, that is the one thing worth remembering — not the call itself, but the PDF that will be posted in the morning.
