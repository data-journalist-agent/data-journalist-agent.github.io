7Two players, two spikes

8The one season that was the whole career.

9In 2005, Dontrelle Willis won 22 games for the Florida Marlins, pitched five shutouts, and finished second in NL Cy Young voting — the 8th1310-best player in baseball. 11Three years later, he was the 121,338th-best player in baseball. 14Two years after that, he was out of the majors for good.

15Dana Barros did the same thing in basketball. 16In the 1994-95 season he averaged 20.6 points per game, made the All-Star team, won Most Improved Player, and finished 8th in the NBA in VORP. 17In the fourteen other seasons of his NBA career, he never once cracked the top 50 — his career-average rank was 153.

18These are one-hit wonders, and they are not metaphors. 19They are what you get when you apply a strict statistical filter to thirty years of professional sports: athletes who were elite for one season and never came close again.

The definition

23The rule that finds them.

24The Pudding's 2018 analysis used two rules: one top-20 finish in your career, and no other top-50 finishes. Applied to 251,076 26eligible careers across 8 leagues from 1988 to 2017, it identifies 78 27one-hit wonders — 7.2% of all players who ever cracked the top 20 in their league. 28Roughly one in fourteen elite careers.

29'Top 20' means something slightly different in each sport: basketball uses VORP (value above a replacement-level player), baseball uses WAR (wins above replacement), hockey uses point shares, golf uses money earned, tennis uses year-end ranking points. 30These were chosen because they're the closest thing each league has to a single number that answers 'who was most valuable this year.'

31Statisticians have a name for the underlying phenomenon: regression to the mean. 32When any measurement mixes skill and luck — and a single season always does — the most extreme results are disproportionately luck-driven. 33Next year the skill remains, the luck resets, and the outlier drifts back toward the crowd. 34One-hit wonders are the extreme tail of that drift.

By sport

36Baseball is the one-hit wonder capital.

37The rate of one-hit wonders is wildly uneven across sports. 38In Major League Baseball, 3932 of 206 40qualifying careers fit the strict definition — 15.5%, more than one in seven. 41The NHL is close behind at 13.0%, the PGA at 10.0%. 42Then a cliff: LPGA and NBA at 3.0%, ATP at 2.8%, WTA at 1.9%, and the WNBA at a single one-hit wonder across 120 careers — 0.8%.

43Some of the gap is talent-pool size: MLB ranks hundreds more players than the NBA each year, so the ranks below 20 stretch much further down. 44Some is about the noisy single-number stats used in each sport: WAR and point shares swing year-to-year more than VORP does. 45Some is structural: the WNBA, the smallest league by historical data coverage, has compressed talent and a narrower top that players bounce around within.

46However you explain it, the bottom line is that 'one-hit wonder' is primarily a baseball and hockey story. 47Half of all one-hit wonders in the dataset come from MLB alone.

49They didn't peak at #1

50They peaked at #17.

51The stereotype of a one-hit wonder is a rookie sensation crashing from the very top. 52The data don't support it. 53Of the 78 one-hit wonders, only 4 54(5%) peaked in the top 5 of their league. 5515 peaked 6-10. 5625 peaked 11-15. A full 34 57— 44% of the total — peaked somewhere between rank 16 and 20. 58The typical one-hit wonder barely cleared the elite threshold.

59Rank 17 was the single most common peak (9 players), followed by rank 19 (8) and rank 20 (7). 60Only three peaked at rank 4 — Alex Zhamnov (NHL, 1995), Darin Erstad (MLB, 2000), Mark Prior (MLB, 2003) — and exactly one at rank 5: Chase Headley, in 2012.

61The right mental model is not 'MVP fell off a cliff.' It is 'rotational player had one honest All-Star season and went back to being a rotational player.'

When it happened

64The peak usually came in year 4, 5, or 6.

65And most one-hit wonders were not rookies at all. 66The peak season landed in career year 4-6 for 6725 of 78 68players (32%), and in year 7-10 for another 21 (27%). 69Only 9 had their best year as rookies. 70The median peak was a player's fifth season, with a standard deviation of 3.4 years.

71They also weren't short-career players who flamed out. 72The 78 one-hit wonders played a mean of 12.5 73seasons — only slightly shorter than the 13.3-season mean of other top-20 careers. 74Rich Beem played 20 seasons; Omar Vizquel played 24. 75One-hit wonders, on average, were not blink-and-you-miss-them. 76They were durable pros who spent a decade or more at journeyman level with one clean spike in the middle.

The fall

78How far do they fall?

79The gap between peak year and a one-hit wonder's typical season is the statistical signature of the phenomenon. 80The median one-hit wonder peaked at rank 15 and held a career-median rank near 200 — an 185-place gap. 81Within-sport, MLB one-hit wonders fall the furthest in relative terms: their median peak-to-typical spike is 8222.9×. 83The LPGA sits at the other end with a 6.4× spike.

The fall is fast. 84Looking at the 75 players with an immediate next-year record, the median drop from peak to the very next season is 105 rank positions. 85MLB one-hit wonders dropped 132 places on average, NHL 118, LPGA 85, NBA 68, PGA 64. 86The median one-hit wonder was essentially not the same player one year later.

The canonical eight

89Meet them — one per league.

90The Pudding's 2018 article highlighted one poster-child per league. 91All eight still qualify when the strict definition is re-applied to the dataset. 92Dana Barros (NBA, peak rank 8 in 1995). 93Dontrelle Willis (MLB, peak 8 in 2005). 94Jiri Hudler (NHL, peak 13 in 2015). 95Rich Beem (PGA, peak 7 in 2002). 96Hilary Lunke (LPGA, peak 16 in 2003). 97Martin Verkerk (ATP, peak 19 in 2003). 98Karolina Sprem (WTA, peak 18 in 2004). 99Tracy Reid (WNBA, peak 15 in 1998).

100Dana Barros's trajectory reads like a chart of a spike you can see from space: 191, 153, 125, 120, 92, 8101, 98, 292, 103, 103, 295, 220, 368, DNP, 211. 102Six seasons of gradual climb, one season of stardom, eight seasons of reversion.

103Dontrelle Willis's curve is sharper and shorter: 104, 109, 8104, 127, 370, 1338, 1123, 1085, 399. 105Three years in the middle go from elite to last-man-in-the-majors, and everything after that is a long tail of minor-league comeback attempts.

106Rich Beem's shape is the golf version — a money-driven spike. 1071999 through 2002: $611K, $250K, $461K, 108$2.94M. 109Then: $1.01M, $230K, $701K, and a slow decline to $17K by 2016. 110One four-day PGA Championship over Tiger Woods restructured a career's earnings without restructuring the golfer.

The extreme tail

111Baseball's biggest outliers.

112Ranked by absolute gap between peak and career-median rank, the top of the list is almost entirely baseball. 113Andres Torres had the largest spike on record: rank 10 in the 2010 MLB on the championship-winning San Francisco Giants, a career-median rank of 848. 114Dave Hollins: rank 19 in 1992, median 719. 115Carlos Pena: rank 17 in 2007, median 481. 116These are not players who were close to elite every year — they were deep-roster professionals who had one season where everything clicked.

117The only non-baseball players to crack the top 10 by this measure are Joachim Johansson (ATP, 2004, French Open semifinalist) and Pascal Dupuis (NHL, 2013, Penguins top line with Crosby). 118Sports with deeper talent pools and noisier metrics produce the most extreme individual outliers.

132What the spike tells us.

133The one-hit wonder has a statistical explanation — regression to the mean, applied to single-season metrics in leagues where the talent gradient is steep. 134It also has a human explanation, which the data cannot reach: a hot streak at the right moment, a perfect system fit, a coach or a teammate that made everything click, an injury that never healed the same way again, a young career that collapsed under the weight of expectation.

135What the data show is that these careers are rare but not vanishingly so — 7% of elite careers — and they are distributed across the span of a career, not bunched at the front. 136The typical one-hit wonder was a professional for more than a decade and was good-but-not-great for most of it. 137One year, against probability, they were great. Then they weren't.

138That's the shape worth remembering the next time someone has the best season of their career. 139It might be the beginning of something. 140Or it might be exactly what it looks like.
