1A 2022 audit of country radio

2Two tomatoes don't touch.

3In a year of country radio across 29 stations and 17 U.S. 4cities, two women's songs landed back-to-back 0.5% of the time. 5Two men's songs did 65% of the time. 6On 25 of those 29 stations, the gap is too big to be chance.

7The 128-to-1 ratio.

8On the 29 country radio stations sampled by The Pudding and SongData in 2022, songs by women artists were played back-to-back 928 times. 9Songs by men artists were played back-to-back 118,861 times. 10That is a ratio of roughly 128 to 1.

11If you turned on the average station at midnight and listened until midnight again, you would hear about 1.6 pairs of women-then-women — and about 205 pairs of men-then-men. 12To hear two women in a row, on average, you'd wait the better part of a workday.

13In May 2015, the country-radio consultant Keith Hill told the trade press that women should be "sprinkled" onto playlists like tomatoes in a salad — never two in a row, lettuce being male artists like Luke Bryan and Blake Shelton. 14The remark sparked outrage and a name (Tomato-gate). 15It also became the most public articulation of a programming rule that, according to Country Aircheck's publisher, "program directors have been telling people not to play two women back-to-back since the 1960s."

16The data below is what that rule looks like, eight years after the public outrage.

22Across 182,848 song plays, men received 81.5% of airtime, mixed-gender ensembles 8.4%, and women just 10.1%. 23That headline number — 10% — has been the focus of public debate about country radio for the better part of a decade.

24But the 10% is only the casting decision. 25Programmers then make a second decision: whether the women they do play get to share airtime with each other. They don't. 26Of the 10% of songs by women, almost none are followed by another woman. 27The b2b women rate is 0.5% of all plays — twenty times smaller than the 10% you'd expect if women were scattered randomly through the schedule.

28Watson's earlier work shows women's share of Billboard's Hot Country Songs 29chart peaked at 33.1% in 1999 and fell to 11.4% by 2015. 30The 2022 airplay number tracks that decline. 31The back-to-back number reveals something the airplay number alone hides: even within the slice of airtime women do get, they are kept structurally apart.

35The coin flip says: not chance.

36To test whether the under-pairing was deliberate, the original analysts shuffled each station's actual playlist 1,000 times — keeping the gender mix exactly the same, randomizing only the order. 37On 18 of the 29 stations, the observed back-to-back women count fell below every single one of those 1,000 random shuffles. 38On 25 of 29 stations, it fell below the 95th-percentile of randomness (p < 0.05).

39The simulation is the closest you can get to a controlled experiment without re-staging the broadcast year. 40It controls for how many women were in the catalogue, how many were currents vs golds, and how many slots existed — and it still finds that women appearing twice in a row is, on most stations, statistically impossible to produce by accident.

41In other words: the under-pairing is programmed in.

The leaderboard.

45Station rates span a 39-fold range, from Los Angeles's KKGO-FM (Mount Wilson, 1.27%) at the top to Cincinnati's WUBE-FM (Hubbard, 0.03%) at the bottom. 46Only six stations exceed 1%. 47Seventeen sit below 0.5%.

48WUBE-Cincinnati played women back-to-back exactly twice in 6,221 sampled plays. 49That is one back-to-back pair per ~3,100 songs — roughly once every ten days of broadcasting.

50The leaders skew toward small, single-station owners (Mount Wilson FM, Cox). 51The bottom skews toward consolidators — Hubbard, Steel City, iHeartMedia, Cumulus — whose centralized programming reaches dozens of cities at once. 52The 1996 Telecommunications Act, by lifting the ownership cap, made it possible for a handful of consultants' rules of thumb to set what 17 markets hear simultaneously.

57Big owners follow the rule more strictly.

58Group the 29 stations by parent owner and the gradient is unmistakable. 59Hubbard (2 stations) sits at 0.12% b2b-women. 60Steel City (2) is at 0.27%. 61Cumulus (5) and iHeartMedia (8) — the two largest country-radio owners in the dataset, between them 13 of 29 stations — are tied at 0.40%. 62The independent and small-owner stations (Cox, COX, Mount Wilson) are the only operators above 0.7%.

63The story isn't that small owners deliberately program women together — it's that they do less of the active separation that a centralized programming desk does. 64Less rule-following looks like more women.

67When women come on, it's late.

68When women do get played back-to-back, when does it happen? 6942.6% of those rare pairings occur between midnight and 6am — overnight, when the audience is at its smallest. 70Another 20.8% air during the 7pm-to-midnight evening block. 71Together, off-peak hours absorb 63.4% of all back-to-back women plays. 72The two prime drive-time blocks — morning commute (15.3%) and afternoon commute (9.3%) — combined hold just 24.6%.

73The rare moments when women are heard together are, by design, the moments when the fewest people are listening. 74The drive-time slots that monetize the station — that pay the bills, that drive Billboard — almost never feature two women in a row.

78The intersection nobody sees.

79Of 182,848 songs played in 2022, 27 were by women of color. Twenty-seven. 80That's 0.015% of all airtime. 81The number of back-to-back plays of women-of-color artists across the entire dataset, all 29 stations, all 19 sampled days, is zero.

82Within women's airplay, the imbalance is stark: 99.85% of plays go to white women, 0.15% to women of color. 83Men of color, by comparison, get 6.6% of all airtime and 695 back-to-back pairs. 84The most under-represented intersection on country radio is not gender alone, and not race alone, but the overlap.

85In 2022 the entire dataset records exactly one back-to-back pair featuring an out LGBTQ+ artist (Brothers Osborne and Hunter Hayes, on Chicago's WUSN-FM, February 7). 86It records zero plays of openly trans or non-binary artists.

91Old hits get to share air.

92Of the 928 back-to-back women plays, 415 (44.7%) are recurrent songs — recent but past their peak. 93Another 309 (33.3%) are gold — older catalogue hits, the Reba McEntires and Shania Twains. 94Only 204 (22.0%) are current releases.

95So when a station does break the rule and play two women in a row, the song most likely to follow another woman is a song from before Tomato-gate. 96Stations are willing to hear women together when those women are already canonical. 97They are not willing, at scale, to hear two new women back-to-back.

98Among the women who do get current-release airplay at all, three artists — Gabby Barrett, Kelsea Ballerini, and Lainey Wilson — account for 52.5% of all current-women plays. 99The on-ramp for any other emerging woman is barely measurable.

101Why the gap matters past the airwaves.

102At every station, the gap between two-men-in-a-row and two-women-in-a-row is enormous: a mean of 64.5 percentage points, ranging from 58.2 (KKGO-LA) to 73.4 (KFKF-Kansas City). 103For every minute of two women you get more than an hour of two men.

104Mediabase airplay drives Billboard's country charts. 105Charts drive festival booking. 106Booking drives label investment. 107The 0.5% / 65% gap is not a soft cultural mood — it is the upstream gate of an industry. 108When women are kept structurally apart on the air, the next contract gets harder to sign, the next chart entry gets harder to earn, the next tour gets harder to book.

109Brittney Spencer, Mickey Guyton, Tigirlily — many of the most public women in country today have described abandoning the radio path entirely, finding alternative gates through TikTok, streaming playlists, or genre-hopping. 110The number above is the reason.

114Eight years later, the rule still runs.

115In 2015, when Keith Hill told a country-radio trade outlet that women should be "the tomatoes" and never play back-to-back, the public response was outrage and reform pledges. 116In 2022, with the dataset open and the methodology documented, the rule that consultant articulated is doing exactly what he said it would do: men 65% of the time, two in a row, and women 0.5% — barely visible to a coin flip.

117The ratio is 128 to 1. 118The p-value, on most stations, is zero.
