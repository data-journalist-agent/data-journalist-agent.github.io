# Two Tomatoes Don't Touch

## Story Spine

**Core claim**: In 2022, on 29 country radio stations across 17 U.S. cities, women's songs were played back-to-back 0.5% of the time — a rate so far below random shuffling that, on 25 of those 29 stations, the under-pairing is statistically certain to be by design.

**Tension**: Most listeners assume that the 10% women-airplay number from earlier studies is the asymmetry. It isn't. Even *given* that 10% share, women's songs are still kept apart from one another at a rate chance can't explain — meaning a programming decision is layered on top of a casting decision.

**Payoff**: When a country station plays a song by Lainey Wilson at 9pm, the next song is almost guaranteed to not be a song by another woman. The "tomato" rule from a 2015 radio-consultant scandal isn't a relic — it's the operating instruction underneath a year of 2022 airplay logs, written in numbers.

## Sections

### edt_01: Hook — Two tomatoes don't touch
**Evidence**: ana_01, ana_12 | **Context**: det_02, det_04

[ana_01] On the 29 country radio stations sampled by The Pudding and SongData in 2022, songs by women artists were played back-to-back 928 times. Songs by men artists were played back-to-back 118,861 times. That is a ratio of roughly 128 to 1.

[ana_12] If you turned on the average station at midnight and listened until midnight again, you would hear about 1.6 pairs of women-then-women — and about 205 pairs of men-then-men. To hear two women in a row, on average, you'd wait the better part of a workday.

[det_02] In May 2015, the country-radio consultant Keith Hill told the trade press that women should be "sprinkled" onto playlists like tomatoes in a salad — never two in a row, lettuce being male artists like Luke Bryan and Blake Shelton. The remark sparked outrage and a name (Tomato-gate). It also became the most public articulation of a programming rule that, according to Country Aircheck's publisher, "program directors have been telling people not to play two women back-to-back since the 1960s."

[editorial] The data below is what that rule looks like, eight years after the public outrage.

### edt_02: The 10% on top, the 0.5% beneath
**Evidence**: ana_02, ana_01 | **Context**: det_03

[ana_02] Across 182,848 song plays, men received 81.5% of airtime, mixed-gender ensembles 8.4%, and women just 10.1%. That headline number — 10% — has been the focus of public debate about country radio for the better part of a decade.

[ana_01] But the 10% is only the casting decision. Programmers then make a second decision: whether the women they do play get to share airtime with each other. They don't. Of the 10% of songs by women, almost none are followed by another woman. The b2b women rate is 0.5% of all plays — twenty times smaller than the 10% you'd expect if women were scattered randomly through the schedule.

[det_03] Watson's earlier work shows women's share of Billboard's Hot Country Songs chart peaked at 33.1% in 1999 and fell to 11.4% by 2015. The 2022 airplay number tracks that decline. The back-to-back number reveals something the airplay number alone hides: even within the slice of airtime women do get, they are kept structurally apart.

### edt_03: The coin flip says: not chance
**Evidence**: ana_06 | **Context**: det_08

[ana_06] To test whether the under-pairing was deliberate, the original analysts shuffled each station's actual playlist 1,000 times — keeping the gender mix exactly the same, randomizing only the order. On 18 of the 29 stations, the observed back-to-back women count fell below every single one of those 1,000 random shuffles. On 25 of 29 stations, it fell below the 95th-percentile of randomness (p < 0.05).

[det_08] The simulation is the closest you can get to a controlled experiment without re-staging the broadcast year. It controls for how many women were in the catalogue, how many were currents vs golds, and how many slots existed — and it still finds that women appearing twice in a row is, on most stations, statistically impossible to produce by accident.

[editorial] In other words: the under-pairing is programmed in.

### edt_04: The station leaderboard
**Evidence**: ana_03, ana_10 | **Context**: det_10

[ana_03] Station rates span a 39-fold range, from Los Angeles's KKGO-FM (Mount Wilson, 1.27%) at the top to Cincinnati's WUBE-FM (Hubbard, 0.03%) at the bottom. Only six stations exceed 1%. Seventeen sit below 0.5%.

[ana_10] WUBE-Cincinnati played women back-to-back exactly twice in 6,221 sampled plays. That is one back-to-back pair per ~3,100 songs — roughly once every ten days of broadcasting.

[det_10] The leaders skew toward small, single-station owners (Mount Wilson FM, Cox). The bottom skews toward consolidators — Hubbard, Steel City, iHeartMedia, Cumulus — whose centralized programming reaches dozens of cities at once. The 1996 Telecommunications Act, by lifting the ownership cap, made it possible for a handful of consultants' rules of thumb to set what 17 markets hear simultaneously.

### edt_05: Owners and the rule
**Evidence**: ana_05 | **Context**: det_10

[ana_05] Group the 29 stations by parent owner and the gradient is unmistakable. Hubbard (2 stations) sits at 0.12% b2b-women. Steel City (2) is at 0.27%. Cumulus (5) and iHeartMedia (8) — the two largest country-radio owners in the dataset, between them 13 of 29 stations — are tied at 0.40%. The independent and small-owner stations (Cox, COX, Mount Wilson) are the only operators above 0.7%.

[ana_05] The story isn't that small owners deliberately program women together — it's that they do less of the active separation that a centralized programming desk does. Less rule-following looks like more women.

### edt_06: When the women come on, it's late
**Evidence**: ana_04 | **Context**: det_06

[ana_04] When women do get played back-to-back, when does it happen? 42.6% of those rare pairings occur between midnight and 6am — overnight, when the audience is at its smallest. Another 20.8% air during the 7pm-to-midnight evening block. Together, off-peak hours absorb 63.4% of all back-to-back women plays. The two prime drive-time blocks — morning commute (15.3%) and afternoon commute (9.3%) — combined hold just 24.6%.

[ana_04] The rare moments when women are heard together are, by design, the moments when the fewest people are listening. The drive-time slots that monetize the station — that pay the bills, that drive Billboard — almost never feature two women in a row.

### edt_07: The quiet erasure of women of color
**Evidence**: ana_08 | **Context**: det_05

[ana_08] Of 182,848 songs played in 2022, 27 were by women of color. Twenty-seven. That's 0.015% of all airtime. The number of back-to-back plays of women-of-color artists across the entire dataset, all 29 stations, all 19 sampled days, is zero.

[ana_08] Within women's airplay, the imbalance is stark: 99.85% of plays go to white women, 0.15% to women of color. Men of color, by comparison, get 6.6% of all airtime and 695 back-to-back pairs. The most under-represented intersection on country radio is not gender alone, and not race alone, but the overlap.

[det_05] In 2022 the entire dataset records exactly one back-to-back pair featuring an out LGBTQ+ artist (Brothers Osborne and Hunter Hayes, on Chicago's WUSN-FM, February 7). It records zero plays of openly trans or non-binary artists.

[CHART: ana_08]

### edt_08: When women do touch, it's the legacy hits
**Evidence**: ana_07 | **Context**: det_07

[ana_07] Of the 928 back-to-back women plays, 415 (44.7%) are recurrent songs — recent but past their peak. Another 309 (33.3%) are gold — older catalogue hits, the Reba McEntires and Shania Twains. Only 204 (22.0%) are current releases.

[ana_07] So when a station does break the rule and play two women in a row, the song most likely to follow another woman is a song from before Tomato-gate. Stations are willing to hear women together when those women are already canonical. They are not willing, at scale, to hear two new women back-to-back.

[det_07] Among the women who do get current-release airplay at all, three artists — Gabby Barrett, Kelsea Ballerini, and Lainey Wilson — account for 52.5% of all current-women plays. The on-ramp for any other emerging woman is barely measurable.

### edt_09: What this means for the music
**Evidence**: ana_11 | **Context**: det_09

[ana_11] At every station, the gap between two-men-in-a-row and two-women-in-a-row is enormous: a mean of 64.5 percentage points, ranging from 58.2 (KKGO-LA) to 73.4 (KFKF-Kansas City). For every minute of two women you get more than an hour of two men.

[det_09] Mediabase airplay drives Billboard's country charts. Charts drive festival booking. Booking drives label investment. The 0.5% / 65% gap is not a soft cultural mood — it is the upstream gate of an industry. When women are kept structurally apart on the air, the next contract gets harder to sign, the next chart entry gets harder to earn, the next tour gets harder to book.

[editorial] Brittney Spencer, Mickey Guyton, Tigirlily — many of the most public women in country today have described abandoning the radio path entirely, finding alternative gates through TikTok, streaming playlists, or genre-hopping. The number above is the reason.

### edt_10: Close — Eight years later, the rule still runs
**Evidence**: ana_01 | **Context**: det_02, det_09

[ana_01] In 2015, when Keith Hill told a country-radio trade outlet that women should be "the tomatoes" and never play back-to-back, the public response was outrage and reform pledges. In 2022, with the dataset open and the methodology documented, the rule that consultant articulated is doing exactly what he said it would do: men 65% of the time, two in a row, and women 0.5% — barely visible to a coin flip.

[det_02] The ratio is 128 to 1. The p-value, on most stations, is zero.

[editorial] Tomato-gate isn't a moment in radio history. It's the operating manual.

## Editorial Notes

- **Exactness rules**: 0.5% (b2b women), 65% (b2b men), 10% (women airplay), 0.03% (WUBE Cincinnati), 1.27% (KKGO LA), 128:1 (men:women b2b ratio), 64.5pp (mean station gap), 25/29 (stations with p<0.05), 18/29 (p=0). All must appear as written, never rounded further.
- **Headline rule**: never let "0.5% women" appear without a paired "65% men" — the gap is the story.
- **Station rule**: KKGO-FM and WUBE-FM should be named on first use with city. They anchor the leaderboard.
- **Don't moralize**: the data is damning enough. Avoid "shocking" / "horrifying" / "sexist." Let the numbers do it.
- **Caveats to weave in**: 19–20 sampled days, not the full year (mention once); 29 stations in 16 markets, not all of country radio; race coding by dataset authors. Do NOT footnote — fold into prose.
- **Tone**: investigative, calm, mathematical. The piece is an audit, not a polemic.
